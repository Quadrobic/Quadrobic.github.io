A Shadow at the Edge of Sight

△

Misha woke four minutes before the alarm. He almost always did—the body had learned the rhythm before the mind could interfere, and now, unasked and without pleasure, opened his eyes at half past six, like some inner spring wound once and for all, with not the slightest connection to his will.

He lay a while longer, looking at the ceiling. It was white, even, without cracks or stains—the ceiling a man of thirty-three ought to have when he could complain of nothing in particular. Misha did not complain. There was nothing to complain of. A ninth-floor apartment in one of those buildings put up in the nineties—neither beautiful nor ugly, simply gray, like everything surrounding his life without attracting or repelling him. Enough furniture. Working appliances. A salary that meant he needn't count his change at the shop, but not one that invited thoughts of anything beyond another holiday, which he spent much as he spent his weekdays, only without an alarm.

He got up, went into the kitchen, switched on the kettle. Outside was November—not the November of storms and first snow, but the one that simply hangs over a city in a gray, sticky veil from which neither clothes nor closed windows offer shelter. The sky merged with the buildings, the buildings with the asphalt, the asphalt with the faces of passersby, and together they formed such a monotonous, inescapable mass that the city seemed covered by an enormous dome of cloudy glass, admitting neither real light nor real darkness.

Misha poured his tea, dropped in a teabag—green, no flavorings—and sat at the table. He wasn't hungry. He was never hungry in the morning, but made himself eat because it was the proper thing, because lunch wasn't until two and he had to work until then, and working on an empty stomach meant feeling that faint, nagging irritation by noon, which would seep like damp into every crack of the day and make it unbearable. He ate a cheese sandwich without tasting it and went to get dressed.

On the landing by the lift he ran into his upstairs neighbor—Polikarpich, as the old man called himself, though according to his passport he was apparently Porfiry. Polikarpich was one of those old men who don't age but dry out, growing harder, drier, more unyielding with the years, like a root no one can pull up. He stood by the lift in sweatpants and a stretched-out checked shirt, holding a string bag with two loaves of bread and a carton of kefir.

“Ah, Misha,” he said, not greeting him so much as stating a fact. “Off to work again?”

“Again, Porfiry Ivanych.”

“And what of it?” Polikarpich looked at him with that peculiar expression of old men who have long since stopped expecting anything from life but still want someone to live up to their expectations. “Any joy in it at all?”

“Some,” Misha said. “Same as everyone.”

“Same as everyone,” Polikarpich repeated, and it sounded like a sentence. “Same as everyone means none at all. I might go fishing today. Bream are biting.”

“In November?”

“Bream bite in any weather, Misha. Makes no difference to them. But it does to us. Can't let it make no difference. You'll pine away.”

The lift arrived. Misha nodded to Polikarpich, stepped inside, pressed the ground-floor button. The doors closed. In the mirror on the lift wall he saw his face—ordinary, neither kind nor unkind, a little drawn, with shadows under the eyes that seemed deeper lately. You'll pine away echoed in his head. For all the old man's eccentricities, Misha thought, he sometimes said things that struck home not because they were clever but because they were exact.

◆

Getting to work took twenty-two minutes on foot and another ten on the metro if he was unlucky with the train. He usually was. Misha stood on the platform among people like himself—faces turned into phones, backpacks and bags, earphones in their ears—and thought how strangely life was arranged: millions of people went underground every morning in order to come up again, and did it so long that they stopped noticing the fact of their burial. He did not exempt himself. He didn't notice either.

Opposite him in the carriage sat a woman with a girl of about five. The girl looked out of the window, though outside was only the black tunnel wall, and every so often tugged her mother's sleeve.

“Mum, what's out there?”

“A tunnel,” her mother answered without looking up from her phone.

“And after that?”

“A station.”

“And after the station?”

“Enough questions.”

The girl fell silent, but a minute later began again.

“Mum, why are we going?”

“Because we have to.”

“Where do we have to?”

“Where we have to. Don't ask.”

Misha watched the girl and thought: he had once asked questions like that. It had once mattered to him what lay beyond the tunnel wall. Then it had stopped mattering. He couldn't remember exactly when—at some point, where we have to had become answer enough.

The office occupied a former factory converted into a business center with loft-style ambitions. Brick walls, tall windows, bold black railings on the stairs. All of it was meant to give the place character, but the character was false, like a bankrupt man's smile, and behind the deliberately unplastered brick lay the same concrete emptiness as everywhere else. Misha climbed to the third floor and greeted the security guard—a fat man named Stepan, who in three years had learned only to call him Misha rather than use his patronymic, and found some strange comfort in it.

“Morning, Misha,” Stepan would say without looking up from his monitor.

“Good morning, Stepan.”

“Any idea if they've made tea up there yet?”

“No idea. Probably.”

“Probably, then.” Stepan sneezed. “When you cross the road, tell them there was tea.”

Misha did not understand what this meant, nodded, and went to his desk.

The working day began with switching on the computer. While the system loaded, he got coffee from the machine—the same coffee as yesterday, the day before, a month ago—and returned to his desk. To his left sat Alexei, a project engineer, a talkative, good-natured man who told the same story every day about his cat stealing a sausage, and laughed every day as though hearing it for the first time.

“Misha, can you believe it,” Alexei began before Misha had sat down. “Again. Third one this week.”

“A sausage?”

“A sausage. A milk sausage. I'd only just bought it. Put it on the table. Turn around—gone. And there he is, the ginger one, sitting under the sofa with these cunning eyes, slapping his tail on the floor. Slap, slap.”

“Maybe it wasn't him,” Misha said, sitting down and entering his password.

“It was him. Grease all over his face. I say, what are you playing at? And he goes, meow. So innocent. As if it wasn't him. As if I'd put it somewhere myself and forgotten.”

“Maybe you did,” Misha said without smiling.

“No, Misha. There's a system here. A carefully planned operation. He waits for his moment. He might've been planning it since morning. While I'm asleep, he's already in the kitchen working out the scheme.”

Misha listened, nodded, occasionally put in a “yes” or a “right,” and that was enough. Alexei did not need someone to talk to—he needed a witness. And Misha was a good witness, quiet and convenient.

To his right was an empty place; the young woman who'd sat there had left two weeks earlier, and no replacement had been found. Traces of her remained on the desk: a forgotten stapler, a mug saying “I'm not a night owl, I just work late,” and a little calendar no one had bothered to remove. Misha sometimes glanced at the desk and always felt the same odd thing—not sadness or regret, but a mild astonishment at how quickly a place once occupied by a living person became empty. As though the person had never been.

His work consisted of something he would have described to an outsider, if asked, as “coordinating processes.” This meant receiving emails, forwarding them to other people, receiving replies, compiling spreadsheets from them, sending the spreadsheets upward, receiving revisions, making revisions, sending them again—and so on, around a circle that could have continued forever because none of these acts had any visible, tangible result. But Misha was not burdened by it. To be burdened you had to feel something, and he felt nothing. Work was like air—necessary, unnoticed, tasteless.

Around eleven, as he compiled another report, the internal phone rang.

“Misha,” said Irina Petrovna, his immediate supervisor, a businesslike, even-tempered woman who spoke as though each word had first been weighed on invisible scales. “Have you sent the third-quarter report?”

“Yes. Yesterday, a little after three.”

“Ah, yes. I see it. A few minor revisions. Check your email.”

“All right.”

“And, Misha”—a pause—“there'll be a slight shift in the deadlines. I'll copy you in. We'll need to review the schedule.”

“All right, Irina Petrovna.”

“Good, then.”

She hung up. The conversation had lasted forty seconds. Misha couldn't tell whether she had liked his “all right.” He could never tell. Irina Petrovna was one of those people around whom you never knew where you stood—on solid ground or thin ice—and her face expressed nothing beyond a polite, measured readiness for the next sentence.

At lunchtime he went outside and walked to the nearest canteen. Inside was the usual noise: humming voices, clattering trays, the smell of bread and overused oil. Misha joined the queue and took a tray.

“Borscht,” he told the woman serving.

“Sour cream?”

“Sour cream.”

“Meat patty?”

“A patty. And mashed potatoes.”

“Fruit drink?”

“Tea, if I can.”

“Tea's after lunch. Fruit drink now.”

“Fruit drink, then.”

He sat at a table by the window. Outside, fine cold rain fell—not real rain so much as wet dust hanging in the air, neither rising nor falling. Misha ate without tasting and watched the rain and thought it seemed to have been raining for a month—or two, or all autumn—and it was impossible to remember when it had begun or when it would end, just as it was impossible to remember when his own life had begun in this particular form—with borscht, fruit drink, window, rain.

At the next table two men in work jackets were arguing.

“I'm telling you, Spartak'll go through,” said one, thin, with a mustache.

“They won't,” answered the other, stocky, red-faced. “Their defense is full of holes.”

“Their defense? It's your head that's full of holes. You tell me who's going through.”

“Zenit.”

“Zenit!” The thin man waved a hand. “Last time Zenit went through, they conceded three.”

“That was then. Different story now.”

“Different story,” the thin man mimicked. “Always a different story with you. Same result.”

“What result, according to you?”

“What result. Nothing.”

“Everything's nothing to you.”

“Is it? And what's yours?”

They fell silent, each staring into his plate. Then the thin man raised his head.

“All right. Two–one. Spartak.”

“There!” the stocky man said happily. “Now you're talking.”

Misha finished his patty, returned his tray, and left.

After lunch, emails again, spreadsheets again, conversations in which he took part politely and without depth, nodding in the proper places and saying the proper words. Toward five Alexei came over and leaned on the partition.

“Misha, where are you off to after work?”

“Home.”

“Right. I was thinking—maybe a beer? They've got some good draft in by the Pyaterochka.”

“Thanks, Lyosha. Not today.”

“Suit yourself.” Alexei pushed away from the partition. “Cat's probably stealing his third sausage already. Better run home. Save the family silver.”

He left, and Misha was alone—apart from the empty desk on his right, the stapler, and someone else's mug.

That evening he went home and reheated dinner—he cooked two days ahead, always the same: pasta with sauce or chicken and rice—then ate in front of his laptop, scrolling through a feed in which nothing lingered longer than a second. Afterward he brushed his teeth, went to bed, and fell asleep, so that seven and a half hours later he could wake four minutes before the alarm again.

Nothing special happened that Thursday. Misha sat at his desk finishing his third coffee and going through his work email, most of it mailing lists—company news, seminar invitations, reminders of deadlines he already knew. He usually deleted them unread, with quick, habitual movements of a finger on the touchpad, not lingering over a line. But this time his eye caught.

The email came from some cultural center—Misha couldn't remember subscribing; perhaps he hadn't, perhaps his address had simply migrated from one database to another, as they always did. The subject: “Workshop: Gothic Bird. Black-and-White Drawing.” And below, in smaller type: “For everyone who feels the need to express what has remained unspoken.”

Misha didn't know what had made him stop. Not the subject itself—he'd never been interested in birds, drawing, or anything Gothic. Not the invitation—he didn't attend workshops, considering them an occupation for people with too much spare time and too few real problems. Something else. Some word in the combination—Gothic, black-and-white, bird—answered within him in a faint, barely distinguishable hum, as though somewhere deep down, in the part of him long since switched off for lack of use, a little lever had turned and closed a contact.

He looked at the email for ten seconds, perhaps fifteen. Then closed it and moved on.

“What've you got?” Alexei asked, noticing him motionless before the monitor.

“Mailing list. Some workshop.”

“In what?”

“Drawing. Birds.”

“Birds?” Alexei laughed. “You can't draw.”

“No.”

“What for, then?”

“Don't know. Just an email.”

“Ah.” Alexei waved it away. “I get twenty of those a day. Don't even open them. Delete them straight off. Life's too short to cry over birds.”

Misha smiled—briefly, politely—and deleted the email too. He did not think of it for the rest of the day. That evening in bed he remembered, but fleetingly, without interest, the way one remembers a scrap of someone else's conversation heard by chance. Gothic bird, he thought, and the phrase, strange and out of place in his measured, calibrated routine, hung in the dark for a moment and went out.

◆

The next day, in a meeting, while the project manager monotonously listed the agenda items, Misha caught himself drawing in the margin of his notebook. He didn't remember starting. He simply looked down and saw that the ballpoint, obeying some separate will of its own, had laid a few lines on the yellow paper—sharp, abrupt, coming not from the mind but from somewhere beneath the ribs.

It was a feather. Long, with a stiff shaft and barbs fine as needles branching from it. Neither beautiful nor ugly—simply exact, as though drawn not from memory but from life, as though he'd seen this feather somewhere and now merely transferred it to paper, stroke by stroke. Misha looked at the drawing, surprised—he'd never been able to draw, certainly not freehand, certainly not absentmindedly—and neatly crossed it out so no one would see.

“Misha.” Irina Petrovna's voice brought him back to the room. “What do you say about the deadlines?”

“Deadlines?” He looked at his notebook, but there were only crossed-out lines. “I think we'll meet them. If the contractor doesn't delay delivery.”

“Good. Keep me informed.”

“All right, Irina Petrovna.”

The meeting went on. Misha listened, nodded, took notes—but the hand that had just held the pen still remembered how the lines had felt going onto paper. Not the familiar mechanical motion of writing numbers and addresses, but something else—smoother, more certain, as though the pen knew where to go and he merely stayed out of its way.

An hour later, back at his desk, he took up the pen again. This time a claw appeared—three curved talons gripping emptiness. The drawing was small, about three centimeters, but so definite that Misha found himself admiring it—for a moment, two seconds—before hiding the notebook in a drawer.

“Misha.” Alexei came over. “Kravtsov from Logistics called. Says the delivery note never arrived.”

“It did. I sent it this morning.”

“Says it didn't.”

“Tell him to check spam.”

“I did. He says he checked.”

Misha sighed, opened his mail, found the message, forwarded it again.

“Tell him to check now.”

“Right.” Alexei paused. “Why so quiet today?”

“What do you mean?”

“Don't know. You seem sort of... not here.”

“I'm here, Lyosha. Here.”

“Right, then.” Alexei left, calling over his shoulder, “It's the cat. Cats can sense these things, you know. When a person's not here. Straight in—grab the sausage.”

Misha couldn't help a small grin. But once Alexei had gone, it quickly left his face. Maybe he really wasn't here. Maybe he really was somewhere else.

That evening on the metro he closed his eyes and tried to imagine the bird. Not the drawing—the bird itself. Alive, if the word could be applied to a creature that so far existed only as two separate fragments: feather and claw. He tried to put it together, picture it whole—silhouette, head, wings—but the image would not form. He saw details—the curve of a wing folded along the body; a head turned slightly, as though listening; an eye, round, calm, with a strange, deep clarity—but he couldn't bring them together. The bird slipped away, fell apart as soon as he tried to hold it whole. It was there—and wasn't. It lived in him, but would not let itself be caught.

It was like a word on the tip of the tongue. You know it, feel its taste, shape, weight—but cannot say it. And the harder you try, the farther it retreats.

Nearby in the carriage two students, a young man and woman, were arguing.

“I'm saying the Watch books are serious literature.”

“Serious!” The girl snorted. “They're fantasy.”

“Fantasy's a genre. Serious is an attitude. Have you read them?”

“The first one. Gave up.”

“There you are. Gave up. And you're talking about serious.”

“I gave up because it wasn't serious.”

“Logic.” He shook his head. “Your logic, Nastya, is like our dean's. Same thing: if I read it, I understand it. If I don't, it isn't serious.”

“And you? Do you read?”

“I read. Everything. Even you.”

“Don't read me. Listen to me.”

Misha listened to their conversation, and it was like rain outside a window—the words flowed without touching him or lingering, and in this was its own peculiar, almost comforting rightness. The world talked and talked and talked—without stopping, without meaning, without pauses—and one could hide in the stream of words as in warm, cloudy water where the bottom couldn't be seen and there was no need to look.

But the bird waited. Quietly, patiently, somewhere at the very edge of consciousness, like a shadow falling on a wall when the light has already gone out but the eyes have not yet adjusted to the dark.

Over the following days Misha lived his usual life—getting up, traveling, working, coming home, eating, going to bed—but now a thin dark line ran through it, like a crack in a wall plastered over but not repaired. He thought about the bird. Not constantly. The thought came and went like surf, advancing and receding, and Misha couldn't catch the moment it appeared or vanished. He drew—in the margins of documents, on canteen napkins, on the backs of receipts he found in his pockets. The drawings grew stricter, more exact, and stranger too, though not frighteningly strange, simply different. A feather turning into bone. A wing whose edges dissolved into haze, as though the bird were made not of flesh but of something finer—mist, silence, the memory of something that had never happened. The eye—always one, always clear, always looking straight ahead, without fear or reproach, simply looking.

One Friday evening, going home, he met Polikarpich in the lift again. The old man held a bucket of potatoes—dirty, freshly dug, smelling so strongly of earth that the smell filled the whole car.

“Ah, Misha,” Polikarpich said. “From work?”

“From work.”

“And how is it? Still living?”

“Still living, Porfiry Ivanych.”

“Good that we're living. That's the main thing, you know. The rest will follow. Or won't. Depends how it goes.” He looked at the bucket. “Potatoes. Grandson brought them. From the allotment. Says they're the last. I say, what do you mean the last, it's still October. And he says, November, Grandad. Well, November. How time goes. You don't notice, but it goes.”

“It goes,” Misha agreed.

“You now.” Polikarpich narrowed his eyes. “You've been looking a bit... Not thin exactly, but... How shall I put it. Thoughtful.”

“Is that bad?”

“Who knows? I've been thoughtful all my life. And what? Still carrying potatoes.” He laughed, dryly, briefly, like a cough. “All right. Ninth for you, tenth for me. Mind you don't change your mind.”

“About what?”

“Who knows about what. Just mind.”

The doors opened on the ninth floor. Misha stepped out, hearing the lift close behind him and creep upward toward Polikarpich and his potatoes. Mind you don't change your mind echoed in his head. Strange old man. Strange words. But something in them wouldn't let go.

On Saturday he woke without an alarm—early, at seven, though he could have slept until ten. He lay looking at the ceiling, and for the first time in a long while it didn't seem merely a ceiling. It was like a sheet of paper—white, clean, waiting.

Misha got up, went to the kitchen, switched on the kettle. Outside, the sky was a different shade—not weekday gray but faintly blue, as though someone had added a drop of another color to the muddy paint. Misha noticed and was surprised that he had noticed. He hadn't looked at the sky as sky for a long time. It had been part of the landscape, like asphalt, wires, roofs—merely the upper boundary of space. But today—it was there.

He sat drinking tea, and his thoughts moved quietly, unhurriedly, like people walking down an empty street on a day off. He thought that he was thirty-three, that half his life, with luck, was gone, and that he couldn't remember a single day spent as he wanted rather than as things happened to turn out. It wasn't a complaint—he wasn't complaining. It was an observation, dry and exact as a medical diagnosis: the patient is alive but cannot feel a pulse.

He took out his phone and opened his feed. Scrolled—photographs, memes, news—and suddenly, between a picture of someone else's lunch and a quotation from an unknown philosopher, saw it again. The advertisement. “Workshop: Gothic Bird. Black-and-White Drawing.” The same one. Only now it wasn't in an email but in his feed—apparently someone in his contacts had shared it. Misha stopped. Read it again. “For everyone who feels the need to express what has remained unspoken.”

He followed the link. The page was simple, almost austere: black background, white text, a registration form at the bottom. The description said that an artist whose name meant nothing to him would lead the workshop, that it would take place on Saturday in two weeks, that materials would be provided, that participants should come empty-handed and open-minded. And one more sentence, in bold: “You will draw not the bird you see, but the bird that sees you.”

Misha read the sentence three times. Then put down his phone, went to the window, and looked at the city—gray roofs, wires, the sky a little bluer than usual today.

The one that sees you.

Did anyone see him? Anyone at all? Not in the sense of knowing his name, job title, phone number, but in that real sense in which a bird sees a person, without looking away, without judging or demanding, simply sees. He couldn't remember the last time he'd felt seen. Perhaps never. Perhaps this was what he lacked—not meaning, purpose, joy, but simply this: to be seen.

He went back to the phone and pressed “Register.”

A confirmation appeared. Misha closed the phone, put it on the bedside table, lay down on the sofa, and shut his eyes. Something stirred in the darkness behind his lids—not an image, not a thought, but the premonition of an image, its shadow—and for the first time in a long while he did not fall asleep at once, but lay still for a long time, listening to the shadow as one listens to rain not yet begun but already felt in the air.

At work on Monday everything was as usual. Alexei told a story about the cat, who this time had stolen not a sausage but a piece of butter and then walked round the kitchen leaving greasy prints.

“Butter, Misha!” he said delightedly. “Do you understand? Butter! Who steals butter? It doesn't even taste of anything. It's just fat. But he ate it. Must've spent two hours licking it. I come home—kitchen full of butter, cat full of butter, everything full of butter. Like someone'd been frying potatoes in there instead of keeping a cat.”

“Maybe he's missing something,” Misha said. “Fats. In his diet.”

“His diet! His diet's premium. Three thousand a month on food. And he eats butter. Ordinary butter. Forty rubles a pack.”

“Maybe it isn't about the price.”

“What is it about, then?”

Misha thought. What was it about? He didn't know. But something in the story—about a cat stealing not what cost more but what it needed—felt familiar. Not the content, but the feeling: knowing what you need when that knowledge matches nothing around you—not the premium food, the proper diet, or what is considered necessary.

Tuesday, Wednesday. Misha lived, and within that life, like an underwater current invisible from the surface, another life flowed—quiet, strange, lonely. Every evening when he came home he sat on the sofa and tried to draw the bird. An ordinary sheet of A4 paper, a ballpoint—he had nothing else—and he would begin. It came out strangely well. Not masterfully, no—but the lines fell confidently, as though his hand remembered what his mind did not. Feather. Claw. Wing. Eye. But whenever he tried to bring them together, it came out wrong. Not a bird but parts of a bird laid on a table, like components of a mechanism he couldn't assemble because he didn't know what it looked like assembled.

On Wednesday evening he called his mother. The conversation was short, familiar.

“Misha, are you eating properly?”

“Properly, Mum.”

“Not going hungry?”

“Not going hungry.”

“And wrap up warm. It's raining there.”

“I do.”

“Aunt Zoya sends her love. She remembers you little. You got off your potty and ran across the yard. She says, what a funny little boy he was.”

“I'm still funny, Mum.”

“No.” His mother laughed. “Now you're serious. Too serious. Like your father.”

Silence. Then:

“Are you alone?”

“Alone, Mum.”

“Well, that's all right. People are alone sometimes. Not forever.”

“I know.”

“Good, then. I love you.”

“Love you too.”

He hung up and looked for a long time at the phone, where “Call ended” blinked. Not forever, his mother had said. How did she know it wasn't forever? And what did forever mean, anyway? He'd been alive thirty-three years, and not once had there been a moment that was forever. Everything was for now. For now you work, for now you're alone, for now you're not going hungry, for now you wrap up warm. Forever was a word from another language, one he didn't know.

He took up the pen and paper again. Drew a wing—large, outspread, with veins fine as threads. Then a head, slightly bent, with a short, strong beak. Then an eye. Round, clear, calm. The eye looked at him from the paper and Misha looked at the eye, and between them was a silence neither empty nor tense, but the silence of two people who have known each other a long time and need no words.

But it wasn't the whole bird. Again, they were parts. Misha felt he lacked something—not ability or skill, but a key, a point from which everything else would fall into place of itself. As though the bird were encrypted, and he had the text but not the code.

He put down the pen. Looked at the clock—it was just after midnight. Time for bed. But he wasn't sleepy. He wanted to sit looking at these fragments—feather, claw, wing, eye—and wait. Wait for them to join themselves together.

He fell asleep like that—at the table, pen in hand, amid scattered sheets, each bearing a part of something whole he could not see.

On Thursday something happened at work. A small event, insignificant in itself, but Misha remembered it—remembered it precisely because it was insignificant.

In a meeting led by Vladimir Sergeyevich, the head of department—a large, slow man who spoke as though taking every word from a deep pocket—they discussed a new project. Discussed it at length, tediously, without enthusiasm. Misha sat, listened, nodded. Then, in the middle of another of Vladimir Sergeyevich's monologues about “strategic priorities,” his gaze fell on the window.

Outside, on the ledge of the neighboring building, sat a crow. An ordinary crow—large, gray, with glossy black feathers on its head. It sat still, looking off to one side while the wind stirred its plumage. Misha watched, at first unsure why it had caught his attention—there were plenty of crows in the city. Then he understood: it sat on the very edge of the ledge, at the point where the building ended and empty space began, and something in its posture was neither defiant nor bold, merely utterly exact—as though it knew where the edge was, and wasn't afraid.

Misha looked at the crow, and inside, where his bird lived, something answered—not an image or a thought, but a short, quiet nudge, as though someone had knocked on a door and stopped to wait.

“Misha?” Vladimir Sergeyevich's voice brought him back. “Misha, are you with us?”

“Yes,” he said. “Of course.”

“Then perhaps you'd comment on item four?”

Misha looked at his notebook. Item four. He couldn't remember what it was about. Read his notes—quickly, hurriedly—and said something correct, necessary, offending no one. Vladimir Sergeyevich nodded. The meeting continued.

Misha did not look out of the window again. But for the rest of the day he felt the crow's presence—not that particular crow, but another, more general one—as though it had flown from the ledge to his shoulder and sat there, quiet and invisible, and he felt its weight—slight but perceptible—and it was the first sensation in a long time that mattered.

At home that evening he tried to draw again. Now, besides the fragments, something new appeared—a posture. He drew a silhouette: a bird at the edge of something—a branch, a ledge—at the moment before flight, not yet airborne but ready. Everything was in this silhouette—feather, claw, wing, eye—but fused together, and for the first time Misha felt he was seeing something close to what lived inside him.

He looked at the silhouette and wanted to cry. Not from grief or joy but from an impossible, aching recognition, as though after a long separation he had met someone he'd once known well and forgotten, and now, looking at them, remembered neither face nor name but only what he had felt beside them. The bird in the drawing was his. Not in the sense that he had drawn it—but in the sense that it was him. Or he was it. Or they were one thing, divided long ago, now trying to come together.

But still it wasn't the whole image. The silhouette was only a shadow. He needed the essence. And couldn't find it alone.

On Friday he came home, took off his coat and shoes, and sat at the table at once, without going to the kitchen or switching on the kettle. Took out a sheet. Picked up the pen. And suddenly stopped.

His hand would not move.

Not as before, when the lines came of themselves, without effort. No. Now his hand simply would not move. As though something had run out. Or closed. He sat before the blank sheet and felt that the bird, so close yesterday, had drawn back today. Not left—drawn back, a step, two, watching him from a distance, with something worse than reproach in its gaze—expectation. A patient, silent expectation that said: I am here. But I won't be given for nothing. You must do something. Not draw—do. Something real, something that will open the door.

Misha put down the pen. Stood. Went to the window. The sky was dark—not November-dark, simply night-dark, and in that darkness was something calm, something beyond rooms, as though another world began outside, a world without offices, spreadsheets, alarms, with only sky and those who could fly in it.

He thought of the workshop. In a week. Saturday. “You will draw not the bird you see, but the bird that sees you.”

Perhaps that was the key. Not pen or paper or his solitary evening attempts, but a place where someone would show him how. Where someone would say the word he lacked. Where the door would open not from within but from outside.

Misha stood looking into the dark and felt the bird, which had withdrawn, approaching again—slowly, cautiously, as a wild animal approaches when it realizes you won't hurt it. He couldn't see it, but felt—from the warmth, the light, barely perceptible movement of air—that it was near. And waiting.

For the first time in a long while he smiled. Not the smile he wore at work—polite, brief, a tick in the box marked “social interaction.” Another one—quiet, inward, the smile of someone hearing music in the distance, not yet able to make out the melody but already knowing it is his.

He left the window, took his best notebook from the drawer—the one he'd bought six months earlier and never opened—and put it on the table. Beside it he laid the pen. Looked at them. Did not touch them.

Let them lie. Let them wait. Like it.

II

There, Beyond the Mists

△

On Saturday morning Misha woke at six twenty, though he set no alarm at weekends. He opened his eyes and for a few seconds couldn't understand why he was awake: his body didn't know this hour, was used to half past six, to weekdays, to work. But something had raised him earlier, something quiet and persistent, like a house creaking before dawn—not a cry but a whisper, not pain but a premonition.

He lay looking at the ceiling. Outside was darkness—thick, starless November darkness. The room was quiet. Only the refrigerator in the kitchen hummed steadily, like a distant ship in fog. Misha turned his head toward the table: notebook, pen, blank sheet. He'd put them there yesterday as one puts out keys before leaving—so as not to forget. They lay waiting.

He got up, went to the kitchen, switched on the kettle. While the water heated he stood at the window, arms folded. The sky was beginning to turn gray—not brighten, precisely turn gray, as though someone were slowly, reluctantly erasing the black to reveal another color beneath it, neither better nor worse, merely different. Misha watched the slow transformation and thought that today was Saturday, he had nowhere to go, he could stand at the window as long as he liked, and no one would call or summon him or ask about item four. And at the thought that he could, he felt neither happy nor sad but somehow warmer, as when a person enters a cold room and brings no warmth, merely fills the emptiness.

He drank his tea, ate a sandwich—the same as every day, cheese, bread, butter—and went to the bathroom. Looked at himself in the mirror for a long time. His face was ordinary—neither rested nor tired, simply the face of a man who had slept seven hours without dreams. But in the eyes, if you looked closely, was something new—not a gleam, more the shadow of a gleam, like light reflected in water just about to ripple.

Misha combed his hair and shaved—carefully, without hurrying, unlike weekdays when every minute counted—and dressed. Not in work clothes or home clothes, but something between: dark jeans, gray sweater, jacket. He didn't know where he was going, but felt he had to go out. Home was home, but the bird wasn't at home. It was out there, in the world beginning beyond his apartment door.

◆

He went outside. The morning was cold but windless, and so the cold felt not like an enemy but something natural, like the color of the sky or the sound of footsteps on asphalt. Misha walked slowly, without a route, and the city around him was different from its weekday self. On Saturday mornings it seemed to take off its mask: empty pavements, closed shops, the few passersby walking not toward the metro but simply walking—and in all of this was a rare, fragile silence, like the first ice on a puddle.

Misha reached the park—a small one squeezed between two residential neighborhoods, with asphalt paths and sickly birches standing bare in November like markers in an empty field. He sat on a bench. Took out his phone. Opened the workshop page. Read the description again. “You will draw not the bird you see, but the bird that sees you.” Again he stopped at the sentence. Then opened the map—the cultural center was half an hour away by bus, in the old part of town, in a former Palace of Culture. He hadn't been to that part of town for perhaps five years. Maybe longer.

He sat looking at the bare birches and thought: should he go? Just go—see where it was, what it was like. Not to the workshop—that was a week away—but to the place itself. To know, to picture it.

He stood and walked to the bus stop.

◆

The bus took a long time, crossing the whole city. Misha sat by the window watching streets change like frames in a film—first new buildings, glassy, smooth, faceless; then older ones, brick, with stucco and balconies hung with washing; then old wooden houses with leaning fences and little front gardens where dead dahlia stalks still stood. Like a person, the city aged with distance from its center, and in this age was something not pitiable but real—like wrinkles on a face that had lived long and was not afraid to show the traces.

An old woman sat beside him with a bag on her knees. It was tightly packed, smelling of fresh bread and something else—dill perhaps, or parsley. She dozed, rocking with the bus, and every so often started awake for a second—looked out of the window, shook her head, fell asleep again. Misha watched her and thought: here is someone going somewhere on her own business, and the business is hers, hers alone, and no one will take it away because it is small, unnoticed, like this bag of bread and dill, and precisely for that reason alive.

“Getting off at the next stop?” the old woman suddenly asked, opening her eyes and staring at him.

“Me?” He was taken aback. “I don't know. Maybe.”

“I am. Otherwise I'll forget. I fall asleep and forget everything. My stop. My bag. Once I forgot my money. On the bus. Get home—no money. Where did it go? Don't know.”

“Maybe you dropped it?”

“No. I checked. Nothing.” She paused. “Maybe I did drop it. Who knows. Mind you don't fall asleep, young man. You'll probably forget too.”

“I won't fall asleep.”

“Mind you don't. I said that too. And then—there you are.” She nodded, took her bag, and rose with a groan. The bus stopped. She got off, and through the window Misha watched her walk slowly, swaying along the pavement, holding the bag to her chest, and disappear around a corner.

Two stops later he got off too.

◆

The former Palace of Culture stood at the corner of two streets, in the shadow of poplars long stripped of leaves, now thin black crosses against the gray sky. The building was old, two stories, with columns at the entrance and cracked plaster showing red brick beneath. Above the door was a half-erased inscription: KIROV HOUSE OF CULTURE. Below, on a plastic sign, modern lettering: “Palitra Cultural Center. Studios. Courses. Workshops.”

Misha stood before it, hands in his jacket pockets. He liked the building. Not because it was beautiful—it wasn’t beautiful, but old, shabby, with peeling window paint and rusted gutters. Yet it had what his business center, with its brick walls and black railings, did not: something real. Someone had built it for something, and that something—culture, art, people gathering—still lived inside it, as warmth lives in a stove not fired for a long time whose bricks still remember.

He climbed the steps and pulled the door. It opened. Inside was warm and smelled of—he couldn't immediately say what. Paint, old wood, dust, wax—all together, the smell of another world, a world where things were done not because they had to be but because someone wanted to.

In the hall a young woman sat behind a desk, wearing glasses, her hair short, her sweater three sizes too large. She was reading a book and looked up when Misha came in.

“Hello. Where are you headed?”

“I...” Misha hesitated. “I'm registered for the workshop. Gothic Bird. But it's next week. I just... wanted to see. Where it'll be.”

“Oh, the workshop.” She nodded. “Yes, next Saturday. Upstairs, third door on the right. The studio. Want me to show you?”

“If it's no trouble.”

“No trouble. Come on.”

They went up a broad wooden staircase, with creaking steps and banisters polished by thousands of palms. Pictures hung on the walls—everything from children's drawings to serious work—and Misha slowed, without knowing why, to look. One caught his eye: black-and-white, on thick paper. It showed a thin curved branch with three leaves, and on the branch sat a bird. Small, schematic, but alive—not drawn, alive, as though someone had caught an instant and fixed it, and the instant remained.

“Is that by your artist?” Misha asked, nodding at the drawing.

“Maria Alexandrovna?” She looked. “No, a student. Came last autumn. She was talented.”

“Was?”

“She left. For Petersburg. Got into the Academy.” The young woman smiled. “But the drawing stayed. Maria Alexandrovna says a drawing always stays. Even when the person goes.”

Misha looked at the bird. It wasn't like his—not at all. Small, light, defenseless. But it had what his fragments lacked—wholeness. It was all there, from beak to tail, and there was such calm in its wholeness that he felt a stab of something like envy. Neither bitter nor malicious—quiet, like a cloud's shadow passing across a field.

“Here.” She stopped at a door. Third on the right. “Saturday, eleven till two. Come along.”

“I will. Thank you.”

“You're welcome.” She turned to leave and added over her shoulder, “And don't be afraid. Maria Alexandrovna's strict, but kind. And the main thing—she doesn't teach you to draw. She teaches you to see.”

Misha remained in the corridor. The door was closed, but he felt something coming from behind it. Not a sound or a smell, something else, nameless but recognized at once, as one recognizes by their footsteps the person one is waiting for. He stood another minute, then slowly, quietly, as though afraid to disturb anything, placed his palm on the door. The wood was warm, rough. And in that touch—palm to wood, warmth to warmth—was something that tightened his throat.

He took away his hand, turned, and went downstairs.

◆

At home he sat at the table and drew the notebook toward him. Opened a clean page. Took up the pen. Put it down. Took it up again. And suddenly, without thinking or choosing, began to draw.

It was coming. Not as before, in fragments and scraps, but whole. Not the bird—the place where it might sit. A branch. Thin, curved, like the one in the corridor drawing—but different, his. With sharp, angular knots, peeling bark, dark veins. He drew it slowly and carefully, each line falling onto the paper as though remembered rather than invented. When the branch was finished, he stopped. No one was on it. But he felt someone ought to be. Someone who would fly up now and settle. He waited—pen in hand, his breathing slower and quieter—waited as one waits for a bird at a feeder: still, patient, holding one's breath.

But the bird did not come.

He closed the notebook. Put down the pen. Sat looking at the wall. Then got up, went to the kitchen, switched on the kettle. While the water heated he stood at the window. The sky had cleared—briefly, for a few minutes—and in that clearing, that strip of blue between gray clouds, was something promising, like a smile that flashed and vanished, though you knew it had been there.

◆

Monday began with an email.

Misha came to work, switched on the computer, poured coffee—and while the system loaded, noticed an email from Human Resources. Subject: “Company Outing: Team Kayaking.” He opened it without giving it much thought.

“Dear colleagues! On Saturday, November 23, there will be a company outing. Team kayaking. Team formation, staff bonding, and informal interaction in a scenic location on the banks of the Desna River. Attendance is mandatory for all department staff. Sportswear required. Equipment provided. Meet outside the office at 8:00 a.m. Bus departs at 8:15.”

Misha read it again. November twenty-third. Saturday.

He opened the calendar on his phone. The workshop—November twenty-third. Saturday. Eleven till two.

He looked at the phone. Then the monitor. Then the phone again. The dates coincided. Not merely coincided—matched so exactly that it seemed someone, knowing nothing of his plan, had deliberately, methodically, without malice or pity, put a wall across his path.

“Misha!” Alexei's voice pulled him out of his stupor. “Seen it? Company outing! Kayaks! Can you swim?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I can't. Can't swim. Sink like a stone. Will they give me a life jacket?”

“Yes. Everyone gets one.”

“All right, then.” Alexei sat on the edge of the desk. “Kayaks, Misha! That's fun. Went kayaking once as a kid. With my dad. On a river. Dragged the paddle through the water—swish, swish—and that was it. Didn't work. Started going round in circles. Dad says, Lyosha, you're paddling the wrong way. What way? I don't know what way. Don't even know why I'm there.” He laughed. “But it was fun. The atmosphere, nature, maybe a fire. Barbecue?”

“Don't know. It says kayaking.”

“Kayaking's a figure of speech. After kayaking comes barbecue. Always does. Kayaks, barbecue, vodka, songs.” Alexei rolled his eyes dreamily. “A classic.”

Misha nodded without listening. One thing in his head: the twenty-third. The twenty-third. The twenty-third.

He opened his mail and found the registration confirmation from the cultural center. Read it again. “Workshop: Gothic Bird. November 23, 11:00 a.m.–2:00 p.m.” Then the email from HR again. “November 23. Meet at 8:00 a.m. Return approximately 6:00 p.m.”

The day was taken. Entirely, morning to evening. Nothing remained—not an hour, not half an hour. The company outing took everything.

Misha leaned back and looked at the ceiling. White, even, fitted with fluorescent lights humming at a frequency you heard not with your ears but something else—your teeth, perhaps, or the bones of your skull. He looked at that ceiling and thought: there, then. That's that. Didn't work out. These things happen. Another time.

And at first—at that first instant, brief as a flash—he really did think it. Didn't work out. These things happen. Another time. Next Saturday, or in two weeks, a month. It wouldn't be the last workshop. The bird wasn't going anywhere. It could wait.

But an hour or so later, in another project meeting, listening to Vladimir Sergeyevich talk about “areas of responsibility,” Misha suddenly felt something—not pain or anxiety, but as though someone had quietly called his name from far away. Not in a voice—but in the way light fell, a chair creaked, a car passed outside and its sound receded, receded, receded—and in that receding was something irretrievable, something you don't notice until it disappears, and once it has, it is too late.

After the meeting Misha got up, went into the corridor, took out his phone. Opened the workshop page. Looked. Then opened his work email. Looked at the HR message. Then the workshop again. Then email again.

He dialed Irina Petrovna.

“Irina Petrovna, it's Misha. Have you a moment?”

“Go ahead.”

“About the company outing. On the twenty-third.”

“Yes?”

“I have...” He stopped. Had what? What word? Business? Plans? Circumstances? “I have an... event that day. A personal one. Would there be any way?”

A pause. Long as a corridor without doors.

“Misha,” Irina Petrovna said in the voice that says no before the word is spoken. “This is a company outing. Attendance is mandatory for everyone. Vladimir Sergeyevich will be there, management will be there. A team-building event. You understand.”

“I understand.”

“It isn't my whim. It's management's decision. And frankly I would not like my department to be less than fully represented.”

“Yes, of course. I understand.”

“Good, then.” A pause. “Misha, if you have something personal—reschedule it. It's only one day.”

“Yes. Of course. One day.”

“Precisely.”

She hung up. Misha stood in the corridor holding his phone, looking at the wall. Gray, with a fire hose cabinet and an EXIT sign. Reschedule it. It's only one day. Of course. One day. What was one day? Nothing. A speck. A grain of sand in a desert where every grain was the same.

He went back to his desk.

“What's with the face?” Alexei asked.

“What face?”

“That face. Like you've lost something.”

“Haven't lost anything.” Misha switched on the monitor. “Everything's where it should be.”

“You sure?”

“Sure, Lyosha.”

“All right. Only, you know, I can. Help, if anything's wrong. I'm not just cat stories. I can be serious too.”

“I know. Thanks. Everything's fine.”

Alexei stood another second, then left. Misha sat before the monitor, its light on his face, emails and spreadsheets and deadlines on the screen—everything that made up his life—and looked at it and thought for the first time: what if this isn't life? What if it's only a screen? And life is somewhere else, behind it, waiting, and it won't wait forever, and one day he'll turn round—and it won't be there. Or the screen. Or him.

He sighed, opened a spreadsheet, and began to work.

◆

That evening, at home, he sat down before the notebook again. Opened the page with the branch. It was still there—thin, curved, with sharp knots. Empty.

He picked up the pen. Put it down. Picked it up again. Then suddenly, quickly, without thinking, began drawing—not on that page but the next, a blank one. His hand moved by itself, and the lines came differently now—not calm, not certain, but torn and nervous, as though the bird he tried to draw wouldn't be caught, wouldn't sit still, darted about, and he caught its movements but could not hold them.

Something emerged. Not a bird—a bird's shadow. Blurred, broken, with sharp corners and empty gaps. Unlike what he'd drawn before—not feather, claw, wing. It was an attempt. An attempt to grasp what would not be grasped. And in that attempt was so much despair—quiet, invisible, like an underground stream—that Misha put down the pen and closed the notebook.

He sat at the table, thirty-three years old, unable to do anything about it. The age, the notebook, the empty branch, the shadow that wouldn't form a bird. He was like a man before a closed door, knowing something important was beyond it, but without a key, the door locked from inside, and no one on the other side would answer because there too was no one.

Reschedule it. It's only one day.

He got up, went to the kitchen, opened the refrigerator. Stood looking at the shelves—chicken, rice, milk, eggs—and wanted nothing. Closed it. Switched on the kettle. Stood waiting. The kettle boiled and clicked off. Misha poured water into a mug and dropped in a teabag. Sat at the kitchen table. The tea was hot, harsh, tasteless. He drank in small sips and looked out into the dark, where there was nothing.

Only one day.

What if it wasn't only? What if this day was the only one and there would be no other? Not because the workshop wouldn't happen again—it would, of course. But because the bird living in him now would not happen again. It was what it was, at this moment, in this state, in this pain. If he did not meet it now, it would change. Or leave. Or die. Another would come—or nothing would.

But these were only thoughts. Quiet, nocturnal, weightless as mist. In the morning it would be Tuesday, alarm, coffee, metro, office. Everything would go on as before. Because it was easier. Because it was familiar. Because only one day really was only one day. If you didn't think. Didn't feel. Didn't know about the bird.

Misha finished his tea, washed the mug, went to bed. Lay in the darkness looking at the ceiling. White. Smooth. Without cracks. Just the ceiling one should have when there is nothing beyond it.

◆

The next days passed as usual—or almost. Misha worked, traveled, ate, came home, slept. But now there was a barely visible crack in each of these acts, like a crack in a glass still holding water, though you already knew it would sooner or later break.

At lunch on Tuesday Alexei told a new story.

“Misha, can you believe it, the cat sleeps in my hat now. Climbs in and sleeps. I look for my hat—gone. Look everywhere. Getting ready for work, no hat. Think someone's stolen it. Then I look—and there's the ginger one in the corner, asleep on it. I wake him. Looks at me—and won't move. Just lies there. I tell him, that's my hat. And he goes meow. That's it. Won't move.”

“Maybe it's warm there,” Misha said.

“Warm! His home's made of fur. He's warm everywhere. It's not warmth. It's principle. He's chosen, you see. The hat, end of story. No arguing with that.”

Misha smiled. No arguing with that. The cat had chosen the hat. Simply chosen. No thinking, weighing, asking. Chosen, and that was that. Now it was his.

“Lyosha,” he said, “have you ever drawn anything?”

“Me?” Alexei was surprised. “No. School, maybe. Little house, sunshine, tree. Same as everyone. Why?”

“Just wondered.”

“What brought that on? Your workshop?”

Misha didn't answer. Alexei looked at him and shrugged.

“Well, drawing's something, of course. If you can do it. If you can't, it's torture. Tried once for my daughter, something for nursery—to draw a horse. Spent an hour on it. Turned out a crocodile. She looks and says, Daddy, what's that? I say, a horse. She says, no, it's a crocodile. I say, maybe it is a crocodile. She says, no, it's a bad crocodile.” Alexei laughed. “That's my entire artistic career.”

Misha laughed too—briefly, quietly. But the laughter soon passed, leaving what had been there before: a quiet, toothlike ache for something that had not happened and now, it seemed, never would.

◆

On Wednesday evening Misha called the cultural center. The same young woman answered—the one with glasses and short hair. He recognized her voice.

“Hello. I'm registered for Gothic Bird. On the twenty-third.”

“Yes, of course. Misha... Misha Sergeyevich?”

“Yes.”

“Is something wrong?”

“I...” He fell silent. How to say it? How to explain? “I won't be able to come. That day. Work's come up. Company outing. Can't miss it.”

“Ah.” Neither surprise nor judgment in her voice. Just a fact acknowledged. “That's a shame. We're only holding this workshop once.”

He hung up. Stood holding the phone, looking at it as one looks at a letter already sent and impossible to recall. The bird would wait. Of course it would. It wasn't going anywhere.

But standing in the quiet of his apartment, he did not believe it. Not with his mind—with the place inside where the bird lived, the place now silent. Silent not like emptiness, but like a house its owner has left, still smelling of their presence, with no lights on and no footsteps.

◆

Coming home on Thursday evening Misha found Polikarpich in the lift. The old man had no string bag, wore a dark quilted jacket, and carried a newspaper under his arm.

“Misha,” he said, as always establishing a fact rather than greeting him. “Been far?”

“Been at work, Porfiry Ivanych.”

“At work. Well. How is it at work? Everything good?”

“Fine.”

“Fine is good.” Polikarpich paused. “I went down to the river today. Fishing. Caught nothing, but sat a while. Quiet there. Water's cold already, dark. You sit—and it's as though there's no one. Just you and the water. And the water, you know, keeps quiet. But keeps quiet in a way that makes you understand everything.”

“Understand what?”

“Who knows what. Everything. What was, what will be, what won't. Water says nothing, Misha, but hides nothing either. Like a mirror. Only not for your face, for...” He moved his fingers, searching for the word. “Your insides. Look into water—you see yourself. Not outside, inside. What's in there.” He tapped his chest. “Sometimes frightening. Sometimes good. Sometimes nothing. But always honest.”

The lift stopped on the ninth floor. Misha got out. Before the doors closed Polikarpich said:

“If something's wrong, Misha, go down to the river. Sometimes water helps more than people. Gives no advice. It's just there.”

The doors closed. Misha stood on the empty landing listening to the lift creep upward, toward Polikarpich and his silence. It's just there. Strange old man. Strange words. But this time Misha didn't dismiss them. This time he stood listening to the lift recede, and in that sound—mechanical, hollow, indifferent—was something of what Polikarpich had meant. Something simply there. Hiding nothing.

Friday. The last working day before the outing. The office was a little livelier than usual—people discussing tomorrow, arguing about teams, what to wear, how much to bring.

“Misha,” Alexei came over, “what team are you on?”

“Don't know. Why?”

“What do you mean, why? Teams. They're assigning us. I'm in the third. With Kravtsov and Natalya from Accounts. Want me to ask for you to join us?”

“Don't mind, Lyosha.”

“Well, I mind. More fun with you. Kravtsov's serious, you know. He'll paddle like it's a competition. The Olympics. And Natalya doesn't paddle at all. She'll sit there screaming. Heard she screamed so much on the last outing the kayak tipped over.”

“Seriously?”

“Well, maybe it wasn't her that tipped it, but she definitely screamed. Don't worry, Misha. It'll be fun. Nature, fresh air, the team. When were you last out in the country?”

Misha thought. When? He couldn't remember. Last summer, maybe, barbecuing with colleagues—no, he hadn't gone. Turned it down. Before that? Childhood perhaps, with his parents, down by the river in the village where his grandmother had a house and vegetable garden, where mornings smelled of dew and smoke, chickens walked in the yard, and he, little, chased them without catching them, laughing, not knowing this was happiness.

“A long time,” he said.

“There you go! You need nature. Air. A river. Better than any...” Alexei stopped, noticing something in his eyes. “What's wrong?”

“Nothing.”

“No, seriously. You're sort of... not here. Again.”

“I'm here, Lyosha. Here.”

“All right.” Alexei moved away but turned back. “Eight tomorrow. Don't be late. Wrap up warm. Cold out on the river.”

Misha nodded. Alexei left. Misha sat before his monitor, on it the spreadsheet he had to finish today, and looked at it without seeing a single number. He saw water. Dark, cold, heavy. Water without a reflection.

◆

That evening, at home, Misha stood before the table. On it lay notebook and pen, and beside them what he'd bought on the way home: a bottle of black drawing ink, a dip pen—a real one, metal-nibbed, with a slit at the tip—and a pack of thick paper, white, rough, uneven-edged. He didn't know why he'd bought them. The workshop was tomorrow. So was the outing. Ink and pen lay waiting. Like the bird.

He sat down. Opened the ink. Dipped the nib. And on the blank sheet, slowly, carefully, like someone touching something fragile for the first time, drew a line.

The line was black, thick, alive. Not the even, dead, mechanical line of a ballpoint. This one breathed. Thicker here, thinner there, uneven, irregular, with more truth in those irregularities than in the thousands of even lines he'd drawn in his life.

He drew a second line. A third. The nib slid across the paper and Misha felt—with every cell of his skin, every nerve—how it left its trace, how the ink soaked into the paper, how the line became part of the sheet, not lying on it but growing into it. It was unlike anything he'd felt before. A first time. Like the first breath. The first step. The first word.

A feather appeared. Not the one he'd drawn before—another. Alive. Barbs branching from the shaft like branches from a trunk, curling at the ends into fine sharp hooks. Then a wing. Open, taut, with a sense of strength—not aggressive but calm, like the strength of a river that flows and knows where it flows. Then an eye. Round, deep, dark. It looked at Misha from the paper, and Misha looked back, and between them was silence. Not empty. Full. Silence containing everything.

But again. Again, not whole. Wing, feather, eye. Parts. Fragments. Shards of something whole he couldn't assemble. As though to see the whole bird he needed not to draw it but to become it. Or perhaps to let it go. Or—and this was most frightening—to let it leave, so it could return of its own accord.

He sat before the sheet, the ink drying on the nib, not moving. The room was quiet. Beyond the window—dark. And in that dark, beyond the window, beyond the wall, beyond the city, somewhere far off, was sky. And in that sky perhaps it flew. His bird. The one he couldn't draw. The one that saw him.

Misha closed the ink. Put down the pen. Covered the drawing with a blank sheet so it wouldn't look. And went to bed.

Tomorrow was the outing. Eight in the morning. Bus. Kayaks. River. Team. Vladimir Sergeyevich. Kravtsov. Alexei. Natalya. Paddles. Life jackets. Water.

And the bird—the bird would stay home. On the paper. Under the blank sheet. In the dark.

◆

Misha woke at six thirty. The alarm was set for six forty-five, but he couldn't sleep anymore. He lay looking at the ceiling, listening to the neighbor beyond the wall switch on the radio—quietly, in the background, as people do when they need not music but another voice's presence.

He got up and dressed. Sportswear, as requested: thermal underwear, sweater, tracksuit bottoms, trainers. A jacket—warm, waterproof, the one he'd worn into the forest a couple of years ago, when he still thought going into the forest was normal.

In the kitchen, over tea, he opened his phone. Nothing new in the feed. Weather: cloudy, three degrees, moderate westerly wind. Sunrise 8:12. Sunset 16:19. A short day. Dark. November.

He finished his tea, washed the mug, went into the other room. Stopped by the table. Laid a hand on the blank sheet covering his drawing—feather, wing, eye. Stood a while. Then bent, lifted the edge, looked. The eye looked at him—dark, deep, calm.

“I'll be back,” he said quietly.

He didn't know whom he was addressing. The bird? Himself? The person he'd been when drawing? The person he'd be when he returned? Or no one. Only words to fill a silence growing heavier.

He lowered the sheet, took his jacket, left. Outside the apartment the landing was cool and smelled of someone else's breakfast—coffee and toast. The lift was slow. Misha stood inside looking in the mirror, and the face he saw belonged to a man going to a company outing. An ordinary face. Calm. Expressionless.

But had anyone—Stepan, Alexei, even Polikarpich—looked into his eyes then, they would have seen what hadn't been there yesterday. Not longing. Not fear. Not resolve. Something else—quiet, distant, like a light in the window of a house you pass at night on a train, which goes out the moment you notice it.

III

Against the Current

△

The bus was ordinary—school-bus style, with narrow seats and worn handrails, smelling of petrol, wet cloth, other people's breath. Misha sat by the window halfway down, backpack on his knees. No one beside him. Alexei sat ahead with Kravtsov from Logistics, already talking, heads inclined toward one another—Misha caught fragments: deadlines, deliveries, contractor—and the words were like the engine noise: familiar, monotonous, meaningless.

Twenty-five people had gathered, perhaps thirty. Misha looked around. Familiar and half-familiar faces, the ones you see every day in the corridor and pass with a nod. Here on the bus they looked different—not because they had changed but because they had been pulled from their usual setting. In the office everyone sat behind their own desk and monitor, creating an illusion of individuality. Here, in the cramped bus, they were together, and together looked the same. Same jackets, same backpacks, same phones in their hands. Same politely patient expressions, like people who have agreed to something they didn't want and can no longer refuse.

“Well then,” someone called from the back. “Let's go?”

“Let's go,” came the answer, and it sounded not like a word but a sigh escaping everyone at once.

The bus moved off. The city drifted past—gray, early, half empty. Misha watched the streets slowly change, as they had that Saturday on his way to the cultural center. Only then he'd traveled alone, toward something. Now he traveled with everyone, away from something. And that away from something lodged inside him, quiet and invisible, like a splinter you can't see but feel with every movement.

“Misha!” Alexei turned over the seat back. “On our team?”

“If that's all right.”

“Already told Kravtsov. He doesn't mind. Kravtsov, you don't mind?”

Kravtsov—a large, shaven-headed man of about forty, with a face that neither smiled nor frowned—turned and looked at Misha.

“Don't mind,” he said. “Can you paddle?”

“Yes.”

“Fine, then.” Kravtsov nodded and turned away. The conversation was over.

Alexei leaned toward Misha.

“See? Doesn't mind. Man of few words, you know. But reliable. Like a paddle—heavy, but effective.”

“Thanks, Lyosha,” Misha said, looking out of the window.

The city ended. Fields stretched beyond the glass—bare, gray-brown, with stubble and scattered islands of frost that had fallen in the night and not melted but simply lain there, like everything in this November—without strength or life, simply because there was nowhere else to go. Fields gave way to copses—birch, aspen, the occasional pine—trees wet and dark, gaps between their trunks filled with nothing, neither sky nor earth, only a gray, cottony emptiness.

Misha watched and thought: in a couple of hours, somewhere back in the city, in the old building with columns and creaking stairs, the young woman in glasses would open the studio door. Switch on the light. Lay paper on the tables—white, rough, uneven-edged. Set out bottles of ink. Put down pens. And people—those who hadn't canceled, who'd come—would take the pens and draw the first line. Black, thick, alive. The first line of their bird.

And he was riding a bus. Past fields. Past copses. Past gray, cottony emptiness.

He closed his eyes.

◆

They traveled for an hour and a half. When the bus stopped Misha opened his eyes. Outside was forest—not a city park with asphalt paths and sickly birches, but real forest: dense, dark spruce climbing a slope, so many trees they seemed one mass, one living body standing silent, and in the silence was something not hostile but estranged, as nature is estranged when people enter it with purposes not its own.

“We're here!” someone shouted.

Everyone stirred, rustled backpacks, clattered down the steps. Misha got off last. His feet sank into wet, loose earth. The air was cold and damp, smelling of needles and rotting leaves. He breathed deeply, filling his chest, and the smell was so real, so unlike the office, that for an instant he felt easier, as though his lungs, tired of air-conditioning and printer dust, had finally received what they needed.

But the instant passed, leaving what had been there: cold, damp, other people's voices.

“Colleagues!” Vladimir Sergeyevich stood beside the bus in a red jacket and rubber boots, looking substantial and confident, a man who knew what he was doing. “Colleagues, attention! The instructor will give the briefing now. Listen carefully. Teams of four. Assignments on the board at the base. Kayaks on the bank. Starting order by lot. Distance approximately three kilometers. No rushing, but no slacking either. The main goal is teamwork.” He paused, adding with a smile meant to signal goodwill but looking more like another box ticked, “And, of course, enjoyment.”

“Enjoyment,” whispered Alexei beside him. “Of what? The paddle? My arms'll fall off straight away.”

“You said you'd kayaked as a child,” Misha said.

“Once. And even then we went round in circles. Three kilometers here. That's paddling and paddling. Might not've paddled that far in my whole life.”

“You'll manage.”

“Of course I'll manage.” Alexei sighed. “Enjoyment's another matter.”

The instructor—a thin, wiry man in camouflage and a knitted cap, his face weathered almost to bark—gave the briefing. How to hold a paddle, how to paddle, how not to capsize, what to do if you did. He spoke monotonously, habitually, like someone who has repeated the same thing many times and long since stopped believing anyone listens.

“The main thing,” he finished, “don't stand up in the kayak. Don't change places. And don't scream. Screaming comes later. Ashore.”

“There you go.” Alexei nudged Misha. “Screaming later. What about Natalya?”

Natalya from Accounts—a short, plump woman with red cheeks and a loud voice—stood nearby and heard him.

“What about me?” she said, offended. “I'm perfectly normal. Last time it was Katya from Marketing screaming. I was quiet.”

“How quiet?” Alexei asked.

“Well...” Natalya considered. “Normally quiet. Enough to be heard, but not too much.”

“You're describing a swimming pool, not a scream,” Alexei laughed.

“You keep quiet. Can't even swim.”

“Me?”

“Yes, you.”

Misha listened without joining in. It was easy, familiar—standing nearby, silent, smiling in the right places. Like a meeting. Like the canteen. Like everywhere.

◆

The riverbank was low, muddy, strewn with old branches and wet leaves. The river—forty meters wide—flowed slowly, heavily, as everything flowed this November: without strength or direction, simply unable to stop. The water was dark—not black, but dark, like strong tea—and reflected the sky, though the reflection was cloudy and indistinct, as though the water reflected not from desire but duty.

Seven kayaks lay overturned on the bank, their wet plastic shining. Misha looked and thought: there. What stood between him and the river. Plastic. Rubber. Aluminum. Things belonging to others, institutional, impersonal, to be used for someone else's purpose—complete a course, achieve a result, demonstrate teamwork. And while he did this, somewhere in the city there was ink, pen, paper. Silence. A bird.

Teams were assigned. Misha was in the third, with Alexei, Kravtsov, Natalya. The draw gave them the second start. While the first team climbed into their kayaks, Misha stood watching the water. There was something hypnotic in its flow—not beauty, but monotony, the same endless repetition as his life: morning, metro, office, evening, sleep. The water flowed the same way—without beginning, end, meaning.

“Come on, let's get going,” Alexei said, dancing with cold. “I'll freeze to death here.”

They lowered the kayak into the water. Kravtsov sat in front, Natalya second, Alexei third, Misha last, steering. The kayak rocked, its bottom whispered across the silt, and as Misha pushed away from the bank he felt it glide—easily, smoothly—and for an instant, one brief instant, thought perhaps it wasn't so bad. The water, forest, cold air, rhythm of paddling—perhaps this was what he needed. Something real, physical, after weeks at a desk.

Then Alexei swung his paddle and struck the water, splashing Misha in the face.

“Lyosha!”

“Oh. Sorry, Misha. Misjudged it.”

“Don't swing it like that.”

“I'm not swinging. I'm paddling. Just...” Alexei swung again, more carefully this time; the blade entered the water with a dull, heavy sound. “There. See? Fine.”

At the front Kravtsov paddled silently, powerfully, in an even, tireless rhythm, like a machine. Natalya paddled awkwardly, rapidly, knocking the side with her paddle. The kayak wandered, veered, and Misha worked the steering paddle at the back to keep their course.

“Left!” Kravtsov shouted without turning.

“I am going left!” Natalya answered.

“No, you're not. You're going right. Left means left.”

“I am paddling left!”

“Left's the other side.”

“I know which side!”

Misha said nothing. Worked the paddle. Looked at water, Alexei's back, Kravtsov's head. Listened to their voices—and did not hear. Because beneath the voices, the splash, the creak of plastic, something else was inside. Quiet. Persistent.

It did not begin at once. First, a thought. Simple, clear, unremarkable: Right now. At this moment. There in the studio. Someone is drawing a bird.

The thought came and went. Misha paddled. The blade entered the water—heavy, resistant—and emerged dripping, and there was something almost meditative in the rhythm, something that might have soothed him but for the thought, which returned. Not as a thought—as a feeling. Physical. In his chest. As though someone had placed a palm there and pressed gently, inexorably.

Right now. At this moment.

◆

The forest closed tighter along the banks. Spruces stood at the water's edge, their thick, knotted roots entwined above the river like hands, and in their tangle was something living, not hostile, but separate. The forest lived its own life, and people in kayaks were neither enemies nor friends, merely something temporary, something that would pass and vanish while the roots remained.

Time moved differently on the river. Misha couldn't tell how long they'd been going—twenty minutes, half an hour—because time here was measured not by clocks but by distances between trees, bends of the channel, changes in the water's color. In this other time the thought of the workshop, at first merely a thought, began to grow. Not sharply, not in a leap, but slowly, as a tree grows, invisible to the eye and unstoppable.

Right now, at this moment, he thought, in a bright room with tall windows, people sat before blank paper. Before them ink, pens, and the woman, Maria Alexandrovna, saying something. Quietly perhaps. Perhaps out of one corner of her mouth, as people speak who know words aren't the main thing, the main thing is the silence between them. And people took up their pens. Drew lines. Lines fell onto paper—black, alive, breathing—and in them something was born that had been inside and now emerged.

And he—he sat in a plastic kayak in the middle of a cloudy river, paddling. Paddling to cover three kilometers of river flowing of its own accord, indifferent to whether he covered it or not. Paddling with people he wasn't close to, toward a goal he didn't care about, on a day that could have been—should have been—different.

“Misha!” Alexei's voice pulled him out. “Why've you fallen behind?”

Misha came to. He had slowed, and the kayak had drifted off course, its stern sliding toward the right bank.

“Sorry.” He worked the paddle, straightening their course. “Thinking.”

“Think later!” Kravtsov shouted from the front. “Paddle now!”

“Yes, sir,” Misha said, and paddled.

But thinking—he couldn't stop thinking. Now that he'd begun, stopping was impossible. The thought grew like a snowball, and something new joined it—not a thought anymore but a feeling, dull, heavy, pressing.

He remembered first seeing the advertisement. Drawing in his notebook margins. Standing at the studio door, palm against the wood. Buying ink and pen. Drawing the first line—black, thick, alive. Drawing the eye—round, deep, calm. And the eye looking at him. Not with reproach. Not with expectation. Simply looking. As one looks at someone long known and awaited.

Now, in the kayak, on the cold river, in a forest not his own, he suddenly understood—not in thought but with his whole body, with the whole place where the bird lived—that he had missed it. Not tomorrow, not yesterday—now, in this very hour, while he was here instead of there. Right now something was happening of which he should have been a part, and he was not. He was here. On the other side. Behind a closed door that would never open because he himself had not come to it.

“Damn,” he said quietly, so no one would hear.

The paddle trembled in his hand.

◆

A kilometer. A kilometer and a half. The forest did not change—same spruces, roots, gray water. But Misha changed. Not outside—outside he was the same: sports jacket, wet hands, paddling rhythm. Inside something was happening. Slowly, irreversibly, like a process you cannot stop because you don't know where the brake is.

First, regret. Simple, clear regret. A shame I'm not there. A shame it didn't work out. Another time. This regret was familiar, habitual, and Misha could have handled it as he handled everything—silently, patiently, waiting it out. But regret passed. Something else took its place—a dull, nagging pain, like an old blow aching with every movement. Not the pain of missing the workshop. The pain of understanding—only now, here on the river—how much it had mattered. This belated understanding was worse than any regret, because regret still leaves hope—another time—and understanding does not. Understanding says: You knew. You felt it. You could have. And you didn't.

Ahead, Alexei paddled ever more quietly. His back, wet-jacketed, shoulder blades showing, moved more and more slowly, and Misha could see he was tired. Kravtsov, by contrast, paddled like a machine—evenly, powerfully, tirelessly—and in his rhythm was something merciless, like a mechanism that does not know fatigue and doesn't want to.

“Lyosha,” Misha called. “How are you doing?”

“Fine,” Alexei croaked. “Fine. Just... a breather.”

“No breathers!” Kravtsov barked. “Keep the pace!”

“Not taking a breather. I'm... adapting.”

“Adapt with your paddle!”

Alexei paddled again, heavily, awkwardly. Natalya was silent—perhaps tired of screaming, perhaps cold. The kayak ran more evenly now than at the start: Kravtsov had set the rhythm, and whoever sat behind had to obey. Like the office. Like everywhere. The strongest set the rhythm; the rest adjusted.

Misha paddled and thought. No longer about the workshop—about himself. How he lived. How his whole life was adjusting. To rhythms, schedules, expectations, mandatory, can't miss it. And he adjusted—quietly, without protest or pain—because adjusting was easier than resisting. To resist you had to know what for. And he didn't. Hadn't known until now.

Or had he? Known, but refused to admit it? Known that somewhere inside him was a bird—living, real, his—and that it mattered more than all the spreadsheets, outings, every mandatory and can't miss it? Known—and boarded the bus anyway?

The paddle felt heavy in his hands, unbearably heavy. Not physically—Misha was strong enough to paddle—but otherwise. Heavy because it wasn't his. Not his paddle, river, rhythm. Someone else's. And holding them, he too was a stranger.

◆

Two kilometers. Perhaps two and a half. The river turned, and ahead through the trees appeared a little bridge—a wooden footbridge spanning the banks. The finish must be close. Seeing the bridge, Kravtsov increased his pace.

“Come on!” he shouted. “Final push!”

“What push?” Alexei groaned. “I'm already...”

“I said push!”

Natalya paddled silently through clenched teeth. Alexei sped up, grunting. So did Misha. Paddles hit the water together—slap, slap, slap—and in the sound was something mechanical, merciless as a metronome.

But something broke in Misha. Not physically—inside. Quietly, like a branch beneath snow: no crack, no cry, just a snap and that was all. He paddled, but his hands worked apart from his body, his body apart from his head, his head apart from everything around him. He was in the kayak and not in the kayak. On the river and not on the river. Here and not here.

He thought about the bird.

Not the workshop. Not ink and pen. The bird. The one living within him, the one he drew at night, whose eye looked at him from the paper. He thought of it, and as the kayak neared the finish the image grew clearer—not sharper, clearer, as though someone were slowly, drop by drop, adding light to a dark room. He saw the wing—open, taut. The feather—long, with fine barbs. The claw—three curved talons gripping emptiness. And the eye. Always the eye. Round, deep, calm.

But in this new light he also saw what he'd never seen before. The silhouette. The entire silhouette, beak to tail. The bird stood on a branch—the very branch he'd drawn—thin, curved, and the bird stood on its very edge, with something in its posture neither defiant nor proud, merely utterly exact—as though it knew where the edge was and wasn't afraid.

He saw it. All of it. Whole. For the first time, whole.

And something in this vision caught his breath and tightened his throat, and his eyes—wet, perhaps from spray, perhaps not—looked over heads, over trees, toward where the river ended and something else began.

“Misha! What's wrong?” Alexei's voice. “We're finishing!”

Misha blinked. Looked ahead. The kayak was already nearing the bank. People stood there—earlier finishers, organizers. Someone waved. Someone filmed on a phone.

“Come on!” Kravtsov shouted, and the paddles struck the water one last time.

The bow touched the bank. Kravtsov stood and climbed out. Natalya followed, heavily, grunting. Alexei groaned, holding the side.

“That's it,” he said. “The end. Can't do any more. I'm dead. Bury me here.”

“Good run,” Kravtsov said, perhaps the longest sentence Misha had heard from him all day.

Misha sat in the kayak. Alone. The paddle across his knees. Motionless.

“Misha?” Alexei looked at him. “What's wrong? Get out.”

“In a moment.” Misha looked at the paddle. Then the water. Then the forest. “In a moment.”

He got out. His feet sank into the muddy bank. Cold. Wet. He didn't feel it. He felt only one thing: the bird. It was here. Not on paper, not in his head—here. Around him. In the forest. In the air. In the silence that came when his colleagues' voices drew away.

“I'll be back,” he told Alexei. “Need to... take a walk.”

“Where?”

“Just into the forest. Ten minutes.”

“All right. Don't get lost. Lunch soon. Barbecue.”

“All right.”

Misha turned and walked into the forest.

◆

The forest met him with silence. Not complete—somewhere a woodpecker tapped, branches rustled, voices carried from far away—but real silence, not city or office silence, the kind in which every sound can be heard because every sound matters.

Misha walked without choosing a route. His feet fell on wet leaves, soft springy earth, roots crossing the path like snakes. Spruces stood straight and even, light between their trunks—not sunlight, there was no sun—simply light, gray, steady, like breathing. Misha walked and breathed, the air clean and cold, his lungs filling with it like vessels long empty, now filling.

He did not think. Analyze. Regret. He walked. Inside was quiet—not empty, quiet, as before something that must happen. Not thunder or an explosion—something quiet. Something that comes when you are ready. And he was ready. Didn't know for what, but ready.

The forest thinned. Trees parted, and Misha entered a clearing—small, ringed with spruces, an island of dead grass in its middle. Like a room, dark green walls beneath the gray ceiling of sky. Misha stopped.

And saw.

◆

It was on the ground. At the clearing's edge, beneath a spruce, on a bed of wet leaves and needles. Small, dark, motionless.

Misha froze. Did not breathe. Did not move. Stood looking.

The bird.

It was his. The very one. Unlike a crow, unlike a jay, unlike anything he'd seen in his life. It came from inside. From the place where it had lived, waited, watched him with its round, deep eye. Gothic, as the advertisement said—but not in ornament or style; in essence. Austere. Black-and-white. With sharp, exact lines, as though cut from darkness and light. Feathers long and stiff, their barbs fine as needles. Wings folded along the body like blades. The head slightly turned, as though it had been looking away and then turned back. And the eye. Round. Deep. Calm.

Misha stood looking, and the world—forest, clearing, sky, voices, river—vanished. Only the bird remained. His bird. The one he'd sought, drawn at night, failed to assemble from fragments, the one that slipped away, eluded him, waited. Here it was. Had found him itself. Had come where bus and paddle and someone else's rhythm had brought him. Had come to him. A wave—not of thought or feeling but something larger, nameless—rose from the depths and passed through his whole body, soles to crown, and Misha closed his eyes, and in the darkness behind his lids was light. Not bright or dazzling, but light, as in a room where a single candle burns and that is enough to see.

Catharsis. Relief. Recognition. As though he'd searched for something thirty-three years and now found it. Not where he'd looked, not as he'd expected—but found it. And in that finding was so much silence that Misha wanted to stand like this forever—eyes closed, the bird before him, the wave slowly, drop by drop, receding and leaving something new. Not joy. Not grief. Peace. Quiet, deep as a riverbed.

He opened his eyes.

The bird was there. Still motionless. Still turned toward him, its eye round and deep.

Misha took a step. Then another. Came closer. Bent down.

And then he saw.

The eye did not blink. The breast did not rise. Feathers that had seemed alive and glossy from a distance were dull up close, dry, pressed against the body like something that no longer gave warmth. Beneath the bird, on the leaves, a dark stain. Small. Dried.

It was dead.

Misha was on his knees—he didn't remember kneeling—looking. The eye, deep and calm from a distance, was empty up close. Not frighteningly empty. Not painfully. Simply empty, like a mirror from which every reflection has gone. The talons he'd drawn—three curved talons gripping emptiness—were clenched, twisted, like those of something that had reached for a hold and failed. The wing feathers—the same fine barbs and sharp hooks—were broken, ruffled, as after a fall.

Misha reached out. Touched it. The bird was cold. No colder than the air, but differently cold. Cold from within. Like stone. Metal. Something that had been alive and stopped. He didn't know how long he sat there. A minute. Five. Ten. Time vanished again as it had in the kayak—but now not into a mechanical, dead rhythm, into silence. The silence afterward. After life. After flight. After everything.

“Misha!” Alexei's voice, distant, beyond the trees. “Misha, where are you? Lunch!”

Misha did not answer. He knelt before the dead bird with nothing in his head. Not emptiness. Not pain. Not grief. Nothing. What remains when something goes and leaves not even a shadow.

He rose. Legs wet, dirty. Hands cold. Looked at the bird one last time. The eye, round and empty. Feathers, broken and dull. Talons, twisted.

“I saw you,” he said quietly.

The bird was silent.

Misha turned and walked back toward voices, people, barbecue, bus, city, office, the life waiting for him in which there was not—and now never would be—room for this bird.

IV

Ash on Canvas

△

The bus was warm. The heater hummed beneath their feet, the windows misted on the inside, and through them the world looked blurred, unreal, like a photograph someone had forgotten to develop. Misha sat in the same seat by the window, backpack on his knees, watching the blurred jumble—fields, copses, wires, poles—everything drifting, merging, impossible to tell one thing from another, and he did not want to.

Alexei sat beside him. Wet, red, pleased.

“Well, Misha,” he said, pulling off his hat and wiping his forehead with his sleeve. “We did it. Three kilometers. Alive. Didn't drown. Natalya didn't scream—well, hardly. Kravtsov's a beast, of course. Reckon he'd have done it alone. For two. For four.” He laughed, loudly, alive, real, and Misha envied the laughter—not bitterly or maliciously, but quietly, as one envies what is lost for good.

“Yes,” Misha said. “We did.”

“Where did you go? I called you. Lunch, barbecue, all that. You weren't there. Thought you'd got lost. Was about to look for Stepan. Our security guard. Then thought, no, not Stepan. Stepan's in town. Who should I look for? Rescue services?”

“I walked. In the forest.”

“The forest.” Alexei looked at him. “And?”

“Nothing. Walked.”

“All right.” Alexei didn't press. He was one of those people who sense when not to ask, though they don't always know why. “Barbecue wasn't bad, by the way. Bit dry, but edible. I had three pieces. Kravtsov five. Natalya one and a half, and regretted it. Says she's dieting. What diet on a company outing? Anti-diet, I told her. Company outing's a celebration. Celebrations mean meat. She says no. Her figure. What figure, I say, you're forty. Took offense.”

Misha nodded without listening. Or listening at a level where words passed through without staying, like water through sand. Alexei talked—barbecue, Kravtsov, Natalya, the cat who'd surely stolen something substantial while they were out, not a sausage or butter but perhaps a slipper—and his words were like the engine noise: steady, monotonous, soothing. They filled the space, and there was something necessary in that filling, because silence was unbearable now. In silence everything returned.

Not the bird. No. The bird remained there, in the clearing, beneath a spruce, on wet leaves. Here on the bus was something else. A sensation. Not thought or image—a bodily sensation, as after a blow when the pain has not arrived but the body already knows it will hurt and contracts, prepares. Misha felt it in his chest, throat, fingertips, and couldn't name it because there was no word for it. Or there was a word but he didn't know it. Or knew it but didn't want to say it.

The bus swayed round a bend. A sign flashed past: “City—47 km.” Forty-seven kilometers. An hour's drive. More, perhaps, with traffic. Misha looked at the numbers and thought: forty-seven kilometers between him and that forest. Him and that clearing. Him and the bird lying on wet leaves, cold, empty-eyed, with no one knowing it was there, no one coming, no one to bury it. It would lie and freeze and rot, its feathers fade, its bones show, and then nothing. Not even bones. Not even memory.

“Misha.” Alexei touched his elbow. “Are you really all right?”

“Really.”

“You seem... don't know. Since the forest. As though something there...” He didn't finish, waved a hand. “Forget it. Just asking.”

“Thanks, Lyosha. I'm all right.”

“Good, then.” Alexei leaned back. “Called home, by the way. Wife says the cat stole a slipper. One. The left. Right one's there, left one's not. Can you imagine? A slipper! Told you—a system. That cat isn't stealing. He's collecting.”

Misha smiled. Or rather moved his lips in a way that meant a smile. Alexei accepted it and turned toward the window.

◆

The city met him with its usual self—gray, wet, indifferent. Misha got off at the office, said goodbye to his colleagues briefly, with a nod, and walked home. On foot, without taking the metro. He walked without noticing the route, as he did each morning—feet carrying themselves, body knowing the way, head far away. Or rather not far, but deep. Inside, where the bird had lived so recently.

Had lived. Misha stumbled—not physically, inside—over the words. Had lived. Past tense. It had lived in him, and now what? Now he'd seen it dead. In the clearing. Empty-eyed. And the vision would not leave. Before his eyes like an ineradicable picture: feathers, talons, wings folded like blades. Silence. The silence afterward.

He went up to the apartment, undressed, went into the bathroom. Turned on the water. Stood under the shower a long time, perhaps twenty minutes, hot water running over face, shoulders, back, and he stood motionless while it washed off river mud, sweat, exhaustion—but not what was inside. What was inside wasn't dirt. Something else. Something water couldn't take.

He came out, dried himself, put on dry clothes. Went into the room. Stopped by the table.

Everything lay as he'd left it. Notebook. Ballpoint. Ink bottle. Dip pen. Pack of paper. And the blank sheet covering his drawing. Feather, wing, eye. That same eye—round, deep, calm. Alive.

Misha stood looking at the sheet, and for the first instant—brief, bright as a flash—felt what he'd felt in the forest on seeing the bird. Peace. Recognition. The wave through his body that left silence behind. The bird had found him. He had seen it. It existed. He wasn't mad or ill—it was real.

But then, like a cold draft beneath a door, something else came. He had seen the bird. But the bird was dead.

Between those two facts—seen and dead—was an abyss Misha looked into only now, beside the table, hair wet, warm sweater on, and from it came a cold unlike anything on the river, in the forest, on the November bus.

◆

He sat at the table. Not to draw—simply sat. Put his hands on it as one puts hands on a table without knowing what to do with them. Sat thinking. Or rather thought and couldn't think, because thoughts would not come into order but moved in scraps and shards, as after an explosion when you cannot tell what came before from what came after.

He thought how the bird had found him. Come to him. Not to a studio, a bright room with tall windows, but to a forest clearing, to a man sitting in a kayak paddling against the current. It had found him dead. As though it had waited for him to come, and when he didn't, died. And then, already dead, came by itself.

It wasn't a thought. A feeling. Irrational, inexplicable, no less real for that. Misha knew—not with his mind but with the place where the bird had lived—that there had been a bond between them. That it wasn't merely an image, a fantasy of an overheated imagination, but something more. Something to do with himself—with who he was, or could have been, or should have been.

And that something had died.

Not in the forest. Not in the clearing. Earlier. Perhaps when he called and canceled. Or boarded the bus. Or said “All right, Irina Petrovna” and hung up. At one of those moments—perhaps all at once—something had broken. The thin, invisible thread running from him to the bird had snapped. Deprived of it, the bird began to fall. Fell slowly, quietly, as a leaf falls in autumn: not swiftly or with a cry, simply releases the branch and drifts down, turning, lands, lies still.

What he'd seen in the forest was no longer flight. It was a landing. The end of the fall. The point where the leaf touches earth and stops.

Misha sat with his hands on the table, the wood warm, rough, real—but he didn't feel it. He felt only what was inside, and inside was empty. Not the emptiness he'd grown used to, comfortable as an old dressing gown. Another one. New. The emptiness that comes when something was there and vanished. When you knew someone was in the room, and now they aren't, the room is empty, you stand in the doorway unable to remember their name or why they left, but you know they were there. Were. And now are not.

◆

The evening passed unnoticed. Beyond the window it had long been dark—thick November darkness—and the room where Misha sat sank into half-light. He didn't switch on the light. Didn't light the lamp. Sat in darkness, neither hostile nor frightening, merely darkness. Like that in the bird's eye.

In the hall, in his jacket pocket, the phone rang. Misha didn't answer. It rang long and insistently, then fell silent. Rang again. Fell silent again. Misha did not move. The sound was distant, as though beyond a wall in another life, and in that other life someone wanted to say something—but he didn't want to listen. Not now.

Half an hour later came a knock. Misha didn't open. The knock repeated, louder, more insistent.

“Misha!” Polikarpich's voice, muffled by the door. “Misha, are you home?”

Misha said nothing.

“Misha, I know you're home. Light's off, but I heard you walking. Or not walking. What are you doing, sitting in the dark?”

Silence.

“All right,” Polikarpich said. “Sit. Maybe I'm wrong. Maybe it's none of my business. But when my wife died, you know, I sat in the dark too. Three days. Neighbors came, put the light on. I tell them, turn it off. They say no, can't sit in the dark. I know you can't. But I sat. You sit too. Only not too long, Misha. Not too long. Darkness is like water. A little's good. Too much—you drown.”

Footsteps. Silence. Polikarpich had gone.

Misha sat in darkness thinking about the old man's words. Darkness is like water. A little's good. Too much—you drown. And also: When my wife died. Polikarpich knew loss. Not abstract, philosophical loss—concrete. A wife. A person. Alive, then gone. Three days in darkness. Neighbors switching on the light because they didn't understand. A man who didn't want light because light was life and life, now, was pain.

Misha didn't know if a bird's death could be compared with a person's. A bird wasn't a person. Not a living creature in the ordinary sense. An image, idea, part of himself. But a part had died. And it hurt—not like the death of someone close, but like losing something without which you are not yourself. As if a hand had been taken away. A hand is not a person. But without it you are different. And this different person is not who you were. Or ever will be again.

◆

He switched on the light. Not because he wanted to—because he had to. Because Polikarpich was right: one couldn't stay in darkness too long. Because the day after tomorrow was Monday, with alarm, metro, office, all that had been his life and would not stop even if inside was empty. The light was harsh, white, merciless. Misha squinted. The room—table, chair, notebook, ink, pen, paper—looked different from in the dark. In the dark everything had been possible. In the light everything was what it was. An ordinary room. Ordinary table. Ordinary things.

He went to the table. Put his hand on the blank sheet—the one covering the drawing. Held it there. Then, slowly, as one does what one does not want but must, lifted the edge.

The eye looked at him.

Round. Deep. Calm.

But different. Misha couldn't say exactly what had changed. Same lines. Same ink. Same paper. But the eye was different. Not empty like the dead bird's eye in the clearing. No. Worse. Dead, but not empty. Something remained—the memory of what it had seen, of something that had been alive and stopped. Like a photograph of the dead: same face, same eyes, nothing behind them. Only memory. Only shadow.

Misha covered the drawing. Lowered the sheet. Pressed it with his palm.

Then sat. Took a fresh sheet from the pack. Laid it before him. Opened the ink. Dipped the nib.

The pen hovered over the paper. Ink—black, thick, shining—gathered at its tip in a drop. The drop grew, grew heavy, did not fall. As though it too could not decide.

Misha looked at the drop. In it, small and black and shining, were reflected the lamp, room, his face—everything in one drop, tiny, distorted, upside down. And he thought: life is like this. In one drop. Everything inside it. About to fall. Spread out. Leave nothing.

He could not draw.

Not because he lacked skill. Not because he didn't want to. Because there was nothing to draw. The image living within him—the one he drew at night, elusive, beckoning, standing on the branch's edge and watching with its calm, deep eye—was gone. Gone. It had died there, in the forest, in the clearing, beneath a spruce, on wet leaves. What remained was neither image nor bird but emptiness. Exact, definite, outlined emptiness from whose shape one might reconstruct what had occupied it, as one might reconstruct a sleeper's face from its imprint on a pillow. But the face itself was gone. Only the imprint. And emptiness.

The drop broke from the nib.

Fell onto the paper—small, black, round—and spread. Slowly, unhurriedly, like blood. Crept through white fibers, filling the pores, and where it passed the paper ceased to be paper. It became a stain. Black on white. Nothing.

Misha watched it spread. Slowly, inevitably. Something in that spreading was neither frightening nor sad, simply final, and he understood: there. That was all. His entire drawing. A black drop on a white sheet. Everything left of the bird.

He didn't wipe it away. Didn't draw over it. Didn't take a new sheet. Sat looking, and the drop—no longer a drop but a blot, stain, nothing—resembled many things. A shadow. A hole. An eye that had stopped seeing.

◆

The phone rang again. This time Misha answered. On the screen: Mum.

“Misha?”

“Yes, Mum.”

“Why weren't you answering? I called.”

“In the bathroom.”

“How are you?”

“Fine.”

A pause. His mother felt—as mothers do, thousands of kilometers away, across wires and towers—that fine wasn't true.

“Misha, has something happened?”

“No, Mum. Everything's all right.”

“Your voice sounds...”

“Just tired. Company outing. Kayaking.”

“Kayaking? In November? Did you fall in?”

“Didn't fall in. Paddled. Tired.”

“All right.” She didn't believe him but didn't insist. “Are you eating?”

“Eating, Mum.”

“And sleep. Go to bed earlier.”

“I will.”

“Misha...”

“What?”

“I love you. You know that.”

“I know, Mum.”

“Good, then. Good night.”

“Good night.”

He hung up. Stood holding the phone. I love you. He heard it every time, in every conversation, and every time answered I know. But today, for the first time, he thought: what did he know? What did love mean? What did it mean to love someone you saw once a year, spoke to once a week—and who was still the closest person you had? Perhaps love was what he'd missed. Not the bird. Not the drawing. This. The ability to feel. To be with someone, with something, with himself. An ability he'd had and, drop by drop, one All right, Irina Petrovna at a time, one canceled registration at a time, had been losing. Had lost. Perhaps not entirely. Perhaps something remained. But little. So little it wasn't enough for a bird.

◆

He called the cultural center. It was late—perhaps nine, perhaps ten—but he knew they stayed open until eleven. The same young woman answered. Glasses. Short hair.

“Palitra Cultural Center. Hello.”

“It's Misha. Registered for the workshop. I...”

“Yes, Misha Sergeyevich. I remember. Is something...”

“Tell me,” he interrupted. “The workshop on the twenty-third. Did it happen?”

“Yes, of course. Today.”

“How... how did it go?”

A pause. She apparently hadn't expected the question.

“Fine. As usual. Maria Alexandrovna led it. About twelve people.” A pause. “Why?”

“Nothing. Just...” He didn't know how to ask. Didn't know what to ask. “Did you draw birds?”

“Yes. The Gothic bird. In black-and-white. As advertised.”

“And... did it work?”

“For some.” In her voice was a gentle, unobtrusive curiosity. “Some did very well. One young woman's was simply astonishing. Maria Alexandrovna said she had talent. Real talent.” She paused.

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome. And...” She hesitated. “Don't be upset. These things happen. Life is long. You'll draw again.”

“Yes,” he said. “Of course. Life is long.”

He hung up. Life is long. She was twenty-something, twenty-five perhaps, and life to her was long, like a river visible to the horizon. He was thirty-three, and knew life was not long. Life was short. Shorter every day. In this shortening life there were—had been—minutes, hours, days that mattered more than all the others together. And one such day, November twenty-third, he had spent in the wrong place. With the wrong people. The wrong way.

And that day would not return. Like the bird.

◆

He sat at the table again. The drop's stain—black, uneven, like an island in a white sea—lay drying on the paper. Misha looked at it and thought.

Thought not as people usually think, in words, but as they think before sleep—in images, scraps, shadows of thoughts. He thought the bird he'd seen in the forest had been his. Not someone else's, not an accident or hallucination—his. A part of him. The part that wanted to come out, wanted to be drawn, wanted to be. And he had not let it. Not because he couldn't—because he hadn't come. He'd chosen bus, kayak, paddle, Kravtsov, Natalya, barbecue. Chosen what belonged to others. Left his own behind. And his own had died.

Now he sat before a blank sheet—apart from the stain—with pen in hand and ink drying in the air, and had nothing to draw. Not because the image had vanished—no, it remained. But as an imprint. A footprint in sand washed away by the tide. You can look at the print, not restore the foot. Look at the emptiness, not restore the bird.

That bird—the one he'd wanted to draw—was particular. His, and only his, born of his silence, his thirty-three years, his apathy and longing. Unique. As every person is unique. It had died unique. And another would not come. Could not. Because another would be another. Not his. A stranger. Misha understood this not with his mind but something deeper, and in the understanding was something final, merciless, like a sentence read in a quiet, unemotional voice—and more frightening than a shout for that.

Perhaps this was a crisis, he thought. Not the magazine kind, buying a motorcycle and changing jobs. A real one. The kind where you understand something is gone for good, and you are not who you were, not who you could have been, and will not become them. Because the way is closed. Because the bird has died.

◆

He left the table. Went to the window. Beyond it—night. City. Lights. Occasional cars. Far away a red lamp blinked on the television tower, like a lighthouse no one needed, blinking because that was how things were arranged, and it would keep blinking even if all the ships had long since sunk.

Misha watched the city and thought that the day after tomorrow was Monday. Alarm. Coffee. Metro. Office. Emails. Spreadsheets. Alexei would tell him about the cat and slipper. Irina Petrovna would call and say All right. Vladimir Sergeyevich would hold a meeting and say strategic priorities. Stepan would say Morning, Misha. Everything would go on as before. As though nothing had happened. As though there had been no bird, ink, pen, clearing, dead eye, emptiness that was now forever.

Everything would go on as before. Because life goes on. Doesn't stop. Wait. Look back. It goes on. And you go with it because there's nowhere else to go, because standing still means dying, and you're not ready to die, though part of you has already died.

He left the window. Went to the kitchen. Took the mug he'd drunk tea from that morning, before the outing. Rinsed it and put it on the shelf. Then—why, he didn't know—opened the refrigerator. Looked. Closed it. Opened it again. Took out cheese. Cut a piece. Ate it standing at the fridge, without tasting. Like morning. Like always.

Then returned to the room. Sat at the table. Before him a sheet with a black stain. The pen dry, ink crusted at the nib. Bottle open. Notebook closed.

He picked up the pen. Turned it in his fingers. Thin, metal, slit at the tip. Simple. Nothing to it. A tool that might have—if only. If he'd come. If he hadn't boarded the bus. If he'd said, No, Irina Petrovna, not this time. If only.

Misha put down the pen. Stood, switched off the light, went to the bedroom. Lay down. The ceiling—white, even, without cracks. Same ceiling. Same room. Same man.

But different.

He lay looking at the ceiling. And in the silence—the silence of night when the city falls asleep and you hear only the wall clock ticking and somewhere far away, beyond the wall, the window, the city, the wind blowing—in that silence was nothing. No bird. No longing. No pain. Only nothing. Quiet, even, bottomless, like dark water without a reflection.

He fell asleep. Outside, snow fell—the first, quiet November snow. Settling on roofs, wires, asphalt, dead leaves in parks, rivers and fields, forests and cities—settling and lying white on black, like ink on paper, shadow on light, memory on oblivion.

And on the table in the dark room, on the white sheet, a stain lay black. Small. Irregular. Like a bird with folded wings.

Or like nothing at all.
