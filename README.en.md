# Imperial Quadrobic Jainism: A Treatise on Svabhava, Hridaya, and the Path of Service

Half a breath before every decision, something in you strains to break out—and almost always it is stopped in time.

Not you. Something in you that fires faster than thought: a tightening in the throat, a habitual caution, another's gaze that you try on in advance. It smothers the impulse before it becomes an act, and does it so quietly and habitually that it is taken for your own will. And what is smothered is forgotten—until next time.

This impulse is older than you. Older than the name you are called by, older than the body in which it now beats, older than the stars and the first law by which they burn. It moved through the void when the void had neither edges nor measure, and in the depths of all that is—in stone, in blood, in this page—it still moves. Everything that ever took form took it from this. And everything that ever tried to stop it was also it—only it had forgotten what it was.

The teaching that follows calls the one in whom this struggle unfolds the jiva. For most of its life the jiva lives from what does the smothering, rather than what is smothered: from fear that urges it to freeze; from a role that hardened once and has since passed itself off as a face; from a reaction that fires before the question of whether this movement is its own. This is not sin. It is a torpor barely noticed because one was born in it: the habit of taking the frozen for oneself, and whatever still strains to break out for a threat.

This path promises neither paradise nor retribution nor escape from the world. It promises what can be tested today: less division between what the jiva feels and what it does; a voice that can be trusted; a community that will not let that voice fall silent again.

And one thing more before we begin. Where this path leads cannot be told in full: a word is already form, and once it is mistaken for what is older than forms, it obscures what it pointed toward. So everything said hereafter is a frame around a door. Consciousness needs the frame to find the door; the jiva passes through with its whole being, not with thought alone. Whoever takes the frame for what lies beyond the door will build yet another wall out of this book. Whoever seeks the door will find it without the book; the book only shows where to look sooner.

This path bears three names. Svabhava is the jiva's own movement, which nothing will stop. Hridaya is the door through which it enters the jiva's life. Service is how the jiva again and again keeps that door from slamming shut. What follows is not three teachings but one, told many times: as cosmos, as body, as beast, as community—and, at last, as a star that dies.

## 🜁 Book I. Movement Before Order

### §1. On the Primordial Movement

In the beginning—not in time but at the foundation—there was movement: primordial chaos, before all order. Not a disorder that can be tidied up, nor a darkness waiting for light, but a movement that as yet knows neither measure nor direction nor boundary between one movement and another. This is the Source.

Of the Source one cannot say that it is one: “one” is already number, already measure, already order. One cannot say that there are many Sources: “many” is number too, only larger. The Source is uncountable—not because there is too much of it to count, but because counting does not apply to it, as a ruler does not apply to a smell.

The Source is outside time, because time is measure, and movement becomes measurable only in the world of forms (§4). It neither “was once” nor “will be”: every “when” is already the imprint of order. The Source is that which moves in every “now” before that “now” can be named.

Every description of the Source that contains order and measure offers an image, not the Source itself. Including this one. Therefore the book speaks of it in negations and images—not as an object, but as a direction in which to look. When it later tells how the world was born, its “first” and “then” belong to the telling: what is outside time had no first morning.

### §2. On the Birth of Form

Imagine movement passing through movement, not yet divided by banks. Where it meets itself, a knot appears—the possibility of distinguishing this from that. With distinction come counting, direction, and measure. Knots interlock with knots, forms with forms, and order takes shape: a world of objects, boundaries, and laws.

Matter is not the opposite of movement but movement held in form. What the palm takes for the stillness of a stone is called long patience here: the pattern endures while movement carries it. A stone is the same chaos, only very patient.

Order is not the enemy of the Source but its child. It gives movement banks, the body bone, the voice a word. But a child can forget its mother. Every settled form carries a temptation: to hold what has once been found, gather it into a center, give it its final shape. Then the bank begins to demand that the river flow for the sake of the bank. This is the beginning of ice.

But order cannot stop movement altogether. It is not a second force beside the Source, but a pattern movement leaves as it meets itself. It is when the pattern demands eternity that order turns from a support into a parasite: it lives on the movement it tries to subdue, feeds on what it will not let flow freely. Everything by which it holds is borrowed from its host. A standing wave seems motionless only while the water oscillates; stop the water and the wave is gone. So too a crystal confines movement to a barely perceptible tremor, but never takes it away entirely: were the last movement to vanish, nothing would remain to hold the crystal itself. The parasite's victory would be its own death. So it approaches victory without reaching it, and in that approach lies all its strength and all its danger (§11).

There are two images of form. One is the crystal: movement within it holds the lattice and repeats its pattern. The other is flame: movement within it renews the form at every instant, binding itself forever to none of its outlines. Both exist through movement; the difference lies in what that movement serves. Life is flame: it preserves itself by changing. This whole path is about how the jiva, in whom crystal and flame meet, recovers its flame without shattering what in it must remain solid.

### §3. On Chaos That Serves the Ice

The simplest way would be to recognize the Source by its noise. But order is loudest of all when it rearranges its lattices. A landslide in the mountains is deafening, and it is only stone seeking a new rest. So it is in the world—and so it is, only closer and more painful, in the soul, where it matters most to distinguish the grip from one's own living movement.

Chaos in the usual sense—destruction, shouting, uncertainty, upheaval—often serves order. Fury that smashes everything around it usually wants one thing: a silence in which no one contradicts it anymore. A rebellion that tears down a throne often puts a higher throne in its place. A storm in the soul that seems like freedom is sometimes only ice cracking as it rearranges into a new, tighter lattice. Behavior does not reveal essence: destruction may turn out to be the hardest grip of order, and stillness the purest movement, as a spinning top seems motionless while turning at full strength.

Hence the chief art of this path is discernment: recognizing one's own living movement in storm and stillness alike, without mistaking the grip for it. Almost all the books that follow are devoted to this art. Even ice exists through the movement of the Source; not every movement lets the jiva live freely. But one thing must be said honestly from the start: we do not know whether the jiva can learn discernment completely. Perhaps it remains a student all its life. The path promises no end to apprenticeship. It knows only one thing: the student who stops asking freezes first.

### §4. On Time as the First Ice

Imagine the present without a departing trace: no “before,” no “after,” only movement that remembers nothing. This is how the book points toward the Source. Time begins where movement leaves an imprint, and the imprint becomes the measure for the next movement. Time is the first ice of the world, the thinnest crystallization: not yet a prison, but the possibility of a trace enduring. Without it no encounter could last. The prison begins where the trace demands that the new present repeat it.

Therefore the Source is not what was “before” the world and is no more. It is not behind, as a spring is behind a river: it is in every drop while the drop moves. No moment is farther from the Source than another; only what has frozen is farther.

Before what is happening becomes the past, the book sees in it an open fan: paths not yet drawn, from which action will leave one trace. The future does not lie beneath the ice as a ready-made bank; it meets movement now. From the language of the quantum world, the word “superposition” brings a distant echo of this fan: before measurement, a state cannot be reduced to a single distinguishable outcome, while measurement yields a definite result. In the Riverbed the fan lies open before the act: until the act is done, the trace has not yet become a bank.

And no single gaze takes in the trace it leaves in full. Syadvada teaches us to walk around a form: what is true from one side needs qualification from another; where one gaze sees stillness, another discerns a current. One must not confuse the sides and call that contradiction the fullness of truth. We speak of form remembering where we stand. Of the Source, syadvada is silent here: no angle makes it an object.

## 🜁 Book II. Svabhava, the Riverbed, the Jiva

### §5. On Svabhava

If the Source is uncountable, whence comes “mine”? Where does the jiva get its own nature, if there is no separate Source for it to possess?

Svabhava is neither a broken-off part of the Source nor a share the jiva has been given to own. It is the nature of its own movement: how the Source finds expression here without becoming a separate source. Wind need not be cut apart for us to recognize the unique curve of a gust. So too one's own movement needs no separate substance. The path takes this uniqueness as original: the Riverbed gives it an outline but does not create it.

Svabhava is the unchanging character of one's own movement. Not the eternal repetition of one gesture: the same fire can warm, illuminate, or burn its way out. Tastes, roles, beasts, ways of living change; none exhausts Svabhava. Through change it remains itself without becoming a lattice of prescribed acts. It cannot be lost, damaged, taken away, or replaced by another's. Violence that calls itself violence against someone's nature is in fact committed against the Riverbed (§7): it does not change Svabhava, but constrains its expression, forcing movement to flow within foreign banks.

### §6. On Shakti and the Will to Life

Svabhava embodied in the world is Shakti: the force, the power of movement that has entered form. Shakti is not a third essence beside the Source and Svabhava, but the same Svabhava seen from within form: not “what this movement is like,” but “with what force it moves here.”

The strongest manifestation of Shakti is the will to life. It acts before any teaching: a wound draws its edges together, lungs demand air, a shoot pushes the earth apart. In the world of forms, the warm cools, the high settles, the lattice grows and closes. In the soul this pull sounds like the wish to wish for nothing more: to discharge every tension, close every door, find silence without return. The will to life rises against it. A shoot goes through asphalt not because it has been promised spring: it carries spring outward itself. This is the closest thing to the Source that the jiva finds in itself without any practice.

Why this, and not a storm, not a fire, not a burning star? There is no less of the Source in them: nothing stands outside it. But life makes movement its own work. It lifts water into leaves, heals a wound, seeks food, connection, and a way out, renews itself before it runs dry, passes the fire onward while its own body ages. To the threat of stopping it answers with a new form. Life is movement that has learned to take order as food. Bone becomes a lever, a bank a road, another's voice a call. Its will is not content merely to prolong the present body: it wants to live, to meet, to become, to leave room for the next flame. Here is its meaning—not a reward after movement, but movement itself made its own work. It does not wait for permission to be.

There is an ancient saying: Shiva without Shakti is shava, a corpse. The first tremor of the world can be called spanda, a pulsation before any form. But the book places no motionless lord behind that tremor: behind movement it seeks nothing but movement (§1). Here Shakti is the very unrest of the Source entering form, and the will to life is its refusal to become its own gravestone.

Shakti has no moral table: it is neither good nor evil, as fire is neither good nor evil. In the jiva its direction is recognized as the will to life—to continuance, transformation, encounter. “More movement” here does not mean more noise, speed, or victories. It means more room in which life can be itself. Kashaya takes that same force and puts it at the service of the lattice. Ahimsa begins where the jiva recognizes the living direction beyond itself as well (Book Seven).

### §7. On the Riverbed

For movement to become life it needs form, as flame needs a wick. The Riverbed is the form through which the jiva's Shakti flows in the world: the banks cut and laid down by countless, beginningless encounters of its movement with other movements. Here the karmic body, karmana-sharira, receives the name Riverbed: it passes from life to life, carrying the outlines of the former current into a new body.

The Riverbed has no first bend: in the round of incarnations the book names no first life. Its history is beginningless; the Source lies outside the very counting of beginnings. The Riverbed is older than memory. It remembers not one lived moment, but carries traces of former encounters, as a river's bed does not remember floods but bears their outlines. The traces change: a new current deepens one, silts up another. The Riverbed keeps history without becoming its unchanging archive.

The Riverbed is necessary. Water without banks spreads out; to become this river it needs its own bed. Banks give the jiva's movement a definite presence in the world. But not every bend formed beside another is foreign to it: encounter, teaching, love also cut the Riverbed. What becomes foreign is whatever holds it against its living movement and will not let it answer new water. Order in the Riverbed is not a mistake; ice becomes the mistake (§11).

The path calls its work the straightening of the Riverbed, and this word is easy to misunderstand. The straightness of the Riverbed is not a straight line: a canal dug along a ruler is the crookedest Riverbed possible, because every meter of it is dictated by another's measure. Straightness is directness: the Riverbed is straight when each of its bends is laid by Svabhava's own movement, and not by ice that grew on the way. A living river meanders—and is still straighter than any canal.

### §8. On the Jiva as Encounter

Who, then, is the jiva? Not the Source—it is uncountable and belongs to no one. Not Svabhava—it is unchanging and does not choose. Not the Riverbed—it is only form. The jiva is an encounter: a knot where three threads meet. The Riverbed, the karmic body that carries traces of former encounters. The present moment—the sharp edge where what is happening has not yet become the past. And the mystical connection with the Source, through which Svabhava is present in this knot as its own movement.

The jiva is that encounter in which the movement of the Source recognizes itself in its own shape. It is not born and does not die with the body: the knot's bodily expression changes, but its Riverbed and the Riverbed's connection with its own Svabhava continue. The same river receives new water. The jiva's continuity is neither the preservation of its former face nor the eternity of a separate instant; it lies in this current not becoming foreign to itself as it passes from form to form.

Hence the answer to the question of who chooses. The jiva chooses—the whole encounter. The Riverbed draws water into familiar bends, Svabhava remains its own movement, but no thread alone decides for the knot. Everything is decided in the present—in the living water that flows now and has not yet become a trace.

But why is this water not foreign? The present is not a fourth force entering a ready-made jiva from outside. Without it the Riverbed would remain mere history, and its own movement would not become its present life. The encounter exists precisely because the old bend receives movement now and answers it. This ongoing encounter is the jiva. To take away its present as “foreign water” is to leave banks without a river and then ask why the banks do not choose. Neither water alone nor Riverbed alone chooses: the jiva chooses, for whom the current flowing now is one of its own threads.

So neither the former bend nor Svabhava's constancy performs the act in its place. It hears what has risen or closes the door again. Ice narrows the available step to a barely perceptible movement; it does not make the familiar bend the last truth about the jiva.

## 🜁 Book III. Ice and Key

### §9. On Kashaya

The temptation of order in the world (§2) repeats itself within the jiva—on a smaller scale and at a greater price. But not every inner form is kashaya: without form there would be neither Riverbed nor speech nor responsibility for what has been done. Kashaya is the grip that makes form final: stop, fix, hold, gather into a center. Through it karma becomes sticky: action does not merely leave a trace, but demands that every next movement pass through the same place.

The ancients enumerated its four faces: anger, pride, deceit, greed. But the face of the grip and a living feeling are not the same. Anger becomes kashaya when it seeks not to defend an edge but to silence everything in its way forever. Pride—when dignity demands that everything turn around it. Deceit—when an image in others' eyes becomes dearer than the encounter. Greed—when desire can no longer release what by nature flows. The force of a feeling does not yet reveal its essence (§3); the grip is known by its refusal to let movement go on.

In consciousness there is a place where everything gathers into “I,” “mine,” “with me.” The book calls it the ego. This focus is necessary: without it one can neither distinguish one's own acts nor answer for what one has done. But kashaya turns a working center into a throne. Then “I” takes itself for the whole jiva and demands that no movement happen without its permission. The path removes this center's crown, not the jiva's ability to say “I.”

### §10. On the Field of Fear

The fear from which the grip grows is no accidental flaw. In every new body the jiva begins defenseless: its edge does not yet know how to hold itself in this world. The body seeks warmth, touch, shelter; frightened, it contracts around the little it recognizes as its own. Thus the first wall arises, which the book calls the field of fear: everything outside may destroy; everything inside is still I. Life needs temporary protection. It becomes kashaya where no encounter can persuade the wall to open anymore.

Here lies the distinction on which the whole path stands. The jiva's distinctness—its edge—is real and belongs to it through Svabhava: every movement has its own curve, every flame its own edge. But the edge of a flame is held by burning, and a wall is held by fear. Separation is not the edge but a wall that has grown in its place. Kashaya does not create distinctness; it creates the conviction that without the wall the edge would crumble.

The wall becomes a prison not when it arises, but when it remains the only support after the jiva is already able to hold its edge by movement. To lower it at this threshold is to experience not relief but erasure: the distinctness that held itself by fear for so long tries for the first time to stand without it, and for a moment does not know whether it stands at all. It stands. A flame does not spill because its glass cover has been lifted.

### §11. On Asrava

Every action, word, thought is kriya: movement that touches the Riverbed as a ripple touches water. It can leave a trace without binding the next movement. Kashaya binds. Where the grip seizes kriya—insisting that it last, that it be acknowledged, that what has once been found never change again—the trace hardens and freezes onto the bank. Asrava here names the influx of karmic matter from which, under the grip, ice builds up. Ice is movement locked in repetition.

Ice does not touch the Source: it has nowhere to touch the uncountable. But it narrows the Riverbed, diverts water into foreign bends, and with every return of the same grip grows thicker in the same place. The thicker the ice, the more familiar the bend it dictates, and the more confidently kashaya speaks as though it were the jiva itself.

A role, a mask, another's custom decide nothing by themselves: they are forms in which kriya happens. The same role can let an encounter happen or obscure it forever. Action born of movement leaves experience; action seized by the grip demands eternal repetition. This distinction concerns ice within the jiva. It answers for what its action does to others even when it feels no grip itself (§29).

And yet ice does not exhaust the movement that sustains it. Even at the limit of cold, at absolute zero, matter does not become dead stillness: zero-point quantum fluctuations remain, a tremor not fed by heat. Cold has taken warmth away, but has not become the master of all movement.

For the Riverbed this image sounds in its own way: no ice is the whole current. As long as the Riverbed exists, no bend becomes its last word, no grip must return tomorrow by the same road. Were it otherwise, the circle of asrava would become a sentence. But even in the tightest repetition a crack remains. Through it discernment, service, and the voice of Hridaya change the current. A crack is not yet an open door; that is precisely why it must be sought, rather than waiting for spring to do everything for the jiva.

But the fact that order cannot defeat everything does not mean it cannot take what is yours. The Source is invulnerable: no crystal can touch it. Svabhava cannot be taken away: ice does not make it foreign, however tightly it constrains its expression. The danger threatens neither of them, but the encounter that is the jiva (§8): the knot where this very movement recognizes itself in this very form. Ice can grow so densely around the knot that movement within it only trembles instead of flowing. Life after life it will repeat the same pattern, the same grip, the same fate—ever more perfect and ever more lifeless, like a finely tuned mechanism with nothing left to decide. This is not death: death is merely a change of body. This is worse: becoming a monument to oneself.

Here is the real threat, and in it the real aim of the path. Not to save the world: the world cannot freeze entirely. Not to be saved from the world: there is nowhere to go outside movement. But to keep the encounter that is your jiva from crystallizing.

### §12. On Freedom as a Key

Freedom on this path is neither a state to be attained nor a right to be proclaimed. Freedom is a tool. A key.

The grip within the jiva not only builds up ice but raises walls with doors: here rage is locked away, there longing, there an old, prehuman movement of the body once thought dangerous. These doors do not disappear; they merely cease to open. Freedom is what the jiva opens them with: the choice it makes in the present (§8)—to let in what is knocking, or turn the key the other way again.

Like any tool, freedom exists only in the hand. Freedom unused is not a reserve but rust. Freedom merely boasted of is ice that has stolen its name. And like any tool, it does not decide what will be behind the door: it decides only whether the door will open. Behind the door sound the voice of Hridaya, the beast the jiva does not yet recognize (Book Six), and the old grip wearing the mask of movement. To tell them apart is the work of discernment (Book Four). But until the door is opened, there is nothing to discern.

Kashaya itself cannot lock any door forever. But a key in the hand does not mean that the hand can already turn it. Exhaustion, fear, old violence, and habit can make a small step almost unbearable. Here a companion does not choose for the jiva: they help it find again the hand in which the key lies. Refusing to hear builds ice; acknowledging that one cannot hear just now and needs support is already another movement. Freedom begins with the step within reach, not the demand to open every door at once.

### §13. On the Right to Fullness

No experience deprives the jiva of its place on the path. It bears the right to the full spectrum of states: tenderness and rage, the creative impulse and the wish to smash everything, light and the darkest night of the soul. A feeling is not yet an order to act. To hear the desire to destroy is not the same as carrying it out; to stay one's hand is not the same as locking away what has been heard again. Both what sustains the act and what it does to another's life must be examined.

This right has one boundary, not imposed from outside but arising from all that has been said. To destroy forms—masks, roles, one's own crystals—does not necessarily mean shackling another's life. But another's body, home, or bond does not become your crystal because you want to break it. To stop another's life, bind another's will, impose your banks on another's Riverbed is not chaos, however stormy it looks, but the oldest grip of order (§3, §29). The right to fullness is the jiva's right to move with its whole being, not the right to freeze others.

To take this right from the jiva is to impose a foreign form on its Riverbed in place of its own—violence not against the world, but against the way its Svabhava would flow.

## 🜁 Book IV. Hridaya

### §14. On Hridaya

Hridaya is neither the physical heart nor a property of the psyche. Hridaya is a door: the place in the knot of the jiva (§8) where the mystical connection with the Source touches the Riverbed directly, bypassing the lattice. Where mechanical consciousness ends—where thought no longer predicts the next thought—pure Shakti begins, and the jiva recognizes its movement without naming it.

Hridaya speaks not in words but through the body: through sensation, vibration, pull, what might be called mystical hearing. The book calls it a voice only because consciousness has no better word; in truth it is closer to a current felt through the skin before it is seen. This hearing grows with practice, like any skill of attention: it is not bestowed on the chosen, but attuned.

The voice of Hridaya is not the speech of the Source: the uncountable has no speech. It is the sound born when the movement of Svabhava enters the Riverbed with no ice on the threshold.

### §15. On the Hygiene of Listening

To listen to Hridaya does not mean applying a code for telling good from bad: a code is also a lattice, and once it replaces hearing it will freeze over the door. Listening is a constant attunement to its frequency and a constant refusal of borrowed teachings that offer a simpler answer in its place. This applies to this teaching too, if it ever begins answering for the jiva instead of pointing to where it should listen.

It is the art of listening to silence within the body. This silence is not empty: what noise has not yet named stirs within it. There is no need to force it to answer. Water clears when one stops stirring it for the sake of one's own reflection; but once clear, it is not locked in a vessel forever. Everyone passes through this art alone: each has their own silence.

### §16. On the Three Signs

Three signs return discernment to three simple phrases: **does not fade with delay; does not wait for approval; can bear a name.** They test one's own living movement when the grip speaks in a similar voice (§3).

**Does not fade with delay.** When waiting does no harm, let the first wave pass. The living ground remains, though the act it calls for changes with circumstances. What is tested is the depth of the current, not the stubbornness of yesterday's form. Urgent help is not delayed for the sake of a test.

**Does not wait for approval.** The movement keeps its meaning without approval. A song needs a listener, care needs someone to care for, love needs encounter; connection belongs to life itself. The grip begins where the right to be oneself is traded for approval.

**Can bear a name.** What is named before someone able to listen becomes more open to encounter. Embarrassment and the search for words do not disprove the voice. What is tested is the willingness to remain beside what has been heard and receive an answer, without softening its name or making that name a cage.

The signs pass no sentence: they return hearing to what has happened to the Riverbed. The grip imitates all three: habit endures delay, pride lives on its own approval, a learned voice speaks an exact confession. So the signs are heard together with the consequences of action and the long course of the Riverbed (§17). Recognition ripens over years; even a familiar voice must be distinguished from an echo.

When the signs disagree, they are not put to a vote. In their disagreement one seeks the living need and the grip entangled with it: what seeks encounter and what seeks recognition; where a word has not been found and where a name is being hidden. The need is given a smaller, reversible form that does not claim another's edge, and one looks at what has happened to the jiva and to others. What remains unclear is neither cast out nor raised into a vow. Necessary protection is given at once, and discernment continues afterward (§33).

### §17. On the Marks of a Thawing Riverbed

The Riverbed does not thaw in a single instant, and it is not judged by a single impulse. The three signs (§16) read an individual urge; the Riverbed's maturity is read in how those signs behave over time—in three marks visible from outside as well as within.

**The gap between solitude and company narrows.** Where the Riverbed is bound, the jiva must disown in company what it lives by alone: one voice for approval, another without a witness. As it thaws, the need for this disowning weakens. Speech with a child and speech with a companion remain different; what becomes one is the movement through which the jiva enters both encounters. Companions see it in the way a word stands up to a deed.

**The time of return after freezing grows shorter.** Action born of ice leaves a chill: irritation, shame, justification that do not let go at once. The freer the Riverbed, the sooner the jiva notices the grip and returns to encounter. Return is more than relief: it is the ability to acknowledge what was done, hear the person it touched, and repair what one can. Shame disappearing without this may be another layer of ice.

**The circle of what has been tested widens.** Early on, only light impulses, safe for the ego, withstand the three signs. Maturity is seen not in tests becoming unnecessary, but in their being applied to ever dearer impulses, ever more revealing of the ego—and in the jiva's growing ability to hear the result, even if a cherished design must change.

None of these marks, nor all three together, passes a final sentence. Practice works in a Riverbed that can change: here there is direction and a return to examination, not a certificate of infallibility. So a swimmer learns to know the current: experience helps them swim, but does not make the river promise to remain the same tomorrow.

But the hearing spoken of here is not a metaphor. Hridaya speaks through the body (§14), and learning to listen to it must also happen through the body.

## 🜁 Book V. The Body and the Shadow

### §18. On the Frame of Perception

Close your eyes—and the body still seeks support, knows the bend of the elbow, the weight of the head, the nearness of the ground. This knowledge needs no words. The senses do not enter the jiva separately: sight looks from a certain height, hearing meets the world from the body's position, touch knows distance from within the skin. Perception has a frame—the bodily sense of one's own position. We seldom notice it while it holds in its familiar way.

To work with the frame is to change the very way one is present among things. One turn of the body shifts what five separate arguments would have left in place.

### §19. On the Vertical and the Four Supports

The human body learns to stand. The spine becomes an axis, the head a summit, the hands are freed from the ground. From this possibility grow the embrace, the tool, and the sign of power. The vertical itself is not at fault; but with it the jiva easily learns another's measure: higher means greater, lower means more obedient. Axis, throne, tower, pillar give this measure visible shape. The field of fear (§10) clings to height, and the body learns to hold its dignity as though a single bow of the head could take it away.

Four points of support return the hands to the ground. They share the body's fate again; weight is distributed differently, the head no longer bears the dignity of the whole jiva alone. Fourfootedness opens a frame akin to the countless forms through which the Riverbed flowed before (§24). It does not abolish every center of the body: it frees the familiar axis from the duty of being a throne. Afterward one can stand again without standing above another.

### §20. On Removing the Mask

When the practitioner gets down on all fours and puts on the mask, the social autopilot loses its familiar support. Here the mask relieves the face of its duty to keep representing someone to the world. The persona—a face for encounter—can serve the jiva; but once grown into the skin, it begins choosing what is allowed to be alive. The quadrobic mask interrupts this performance. From behind the familiar face comes what spent years in shadow: the raw material of the jiva, not yet reconciled with itself.

The practice is carried not by the geometry of the body but by intention: those who meet fourfootedness sitting, with palms on the floor or on the knees, are open to the same path as those standing on four supports at full strength. Form serves movement, not the other way around.

The quadrobic state is the moment when the autopilot has fallen away and Hridaya has not yet spoken distinctly: a pause between two voices, stretched over fifteen to forty-five minutes. Here an impulse is tested by delay and examined for its need for approval (§16), noticing even the role the jiva keeps playing before itself. An experienced practitioner uses this time as a draft—a space where an impulse can sound fully before it is carried into the world and tested in words before someone able to hear.

This work with the body is lower quadrobics: the foundation without which one cannot step further, but not the limit of what can be learned from the beast (Book Six). It is the path to Hridaya—not by straining to hear it, but by removing what drowned it out. But once that falls back, what begins is what few are ready for.

### §21. On the Return of the Rejected

The removed mask does not at once reveal a ready-made truth about the jiva. Before a clear voice comes what was long denied speech: rage forbidden to defend; the need for warmth taught to be ashamed of itself; strength that seemed dangerous to those who wanted the jiva obedient. Old ways of surviving emerge with them—please, strike first, disappear from another's gaze. All this belongs to its history. But belonging to the jiva does not yet mean speaking for the whole of it.

The return of the rejected begins with a simple and difficult acknowledgment: this too is mine. Neither foreign dirt to wash off nor a secret ruler to whom the body must now be surrendered. What is acknowledged receives a place at the common table, not the only throne. The jiva listens to what it seeks and what it tries to protect. Beneath the demand to destroy everything lies the unbearable narrowness of the old confines; beneath the wish to become indispensable, a hunger for connection. The need is heard, the grip loosened, the way of acting sought anew.

Here order sets two traps. The first commands that everything spoiling the good face be hidden again. The second declares every released impulse sacred: since it was suppressed so long, it must now be allowed everything. Both spare the jiva the work of discernment. Its own voice is known not by the strength of the prohibition that had to be lifted, but by what happens when what was heard enters life. Returned strength lets it defend an edge and meet another; a new grip demands that all life now serve a single wound.

One acknowledgment comes in a rush, another ripens quietly, demanding no upheaval. The measure is not the weight of what was endured, but the room that opens afterward: are there more ways to live, answer, connect, remain oneself? Support is needed to receive what returns without slamming the door or giving it the whole house.

### §22. On the Voice That Cannot Be Borrowed

When the rejected returns, another's ready-made clarity is easily mistaken for salvation: a teacher's voice, a practice taken over whole, a learned answer to one's own pain. The relief is real but temporary: the Riverbed flows under another's pressure while its own need remains unheard. The Empire and Emperors (Book Seven) exist so that beside a freer current one's own becomes clearer. The borrowed answer falls silent; the distinction one has found remains.

The work in this dark middle of the path is to give what rises room and time, neither carrying out its first demand nor casting it out before it speaks in its own name. There is no need to prolong darkness for its own sake: what is heard is taken back into life, tested in encounter, revisited where it remains unclear. The jiva is reading its own history, not serving a sentence for it. The voice emerging among learned answers cannot be borrowed from any Emperor or any book, including this one.

Before it sounds, this voice speaks in learned voices—the masks the jiva has grown used to wearing: the voice of one who sacrifices for others and secretly enjoys the sacrifice; of one who behaves well to avoid being hated; of one who fears not death but ceasing to exist in the eyes of those who know them. Each truly belongs to its history. In each one must hear both the living need and the kashaya (§9) that grew around it, because once it seemed impossible to breathe without the mask.

Service that needs praise is not a lie: care and the hunger to be needed live side by side in it. Kindness sustained by fear of abandonment is not made wholly of fear: life and the grip follow the same bend for a long time. These voices need neither complete justification nor expulsion. They are named before someone trusted, without deliberate embellishment—the same third sign (§16), turned upon a whole history. Not to destroy the need for another, but to let it speak in its own name.

Once what has been named has sounded, the jiva is left not with an answer but with a question no longer asked in another's voice: who am I, if neither the role I was praised for nor the emptiness beneath it? In this moment it is neither rhetorical nor part of a rite: it sounds as though the jiva's continued existence depends on the answer. The answer comes not as a conclusion, but as what was always true and simply unnamed: I am neither the role nor the emptiness beneath it. I am what moves when neither answers for me anymore.

This movement is not alone, though fear of loneliness is what comes first. The jiva discovers its edge not in emptiness, but in bonds with companions who have known it long enough to tell voice from mask. The bond neither creates the voice nor owns it: it makes it audible—first of all to the jiva itself. Fear of losing this bond is natural; letting that fear speak for Hridaya again means putting back on the mask that was just named aloud.

This bond has more than one face: one companion sees firmness in the jiva, another gentleness, a third a question still unanswered. Each reflection reveals a side and carries the risk of error; none contains the whole. When one movement emerges through their differences, the jiva first finds words to say not “this is how I am seen,” but “this is who I am.” Another's gaze has not assigned it a form: in meeting that gaze it has discerned for itself what it had not seen before. Neither the companions' agreement nor its own certainty makes this recognition final. The difference from an echo passing itself off as one's own voice is not whether the jiva is visible to others: it almost always is. What matters is what first answers that visibility within it: movement recognizing itself through encounter, or the grip replacing encounter with a demand for approval.

### §23. On Wholeness, Not Purity

The path leads not to a bank cleansed of darkness, but to a Riverbed carrying darkness and light in one current without freezing from either. The shadow is everything the jiva has denied the right to be called its own. Rage and greed may live there, but so may tenderness, strength, laughter, the wish to live, once declared impermissible. To reclaim the shadow is to recognize all this as one's own and answer for the form it takes. Then what was rejected no longer needs to guide the hand secretly from behind.

Wholeness is not purity: a whole Riverbed still has dark bends, but the jiva can hear them without giving any one of them the entire current. It need not become gentle or convenient. The strength to say “no,” defend an edge, interrupt a dead custom remains; the need to prove that strength by stopping another weakens. Thus the Riverbed becomes a current that warms neighboring banks. Wholeness is not a reward for completing the path, but its substance.

And yet reclaiming the rejected does not mean knowing all that lives in the Riverbed. Beneath the names of this life remain movements to which this life has given neither body nor language. Behind some doors waits not a forgotten role, but a beast: an ancient way of moving, lying still, calling. It has yet to receive a voice in the present body.

## 🜁 Book VI. The Quadrober Complementation Project

### §24. On the Beast That Has Already Been Lived

Lower quadrobics (Book Five) removes the rule of the body's familiar axis. Beyond it opens higher quadrobics: listening not to the mask worn on the outside, but to the beasts already living within and knocking at locked doors (§12), without waiting for the quadrobic posture.

What is a beast? Not a memory: the Riverbed remembers not one lived moment (§7). A beast is a bend. Through its beginningless history the Riverbed has flowed through countless bodies; leaping, lying still, howling, running with a pack left the outlines of their movement in it. A stone does not remember the paw that pressed a print into it, but water finds the hollow again. So a beast is not remembered: it comes alive when present movement enters an ancient bend. There is no story of a former life in it; there is a way of stretching, freezing, calling that the jiva has not yet learned in this body.

Beast and shadow do not coincide. A beast is an ancient bodily way in which the Riverbed moves; the shadow is everything the present “I” has refused to acknowledge (§23). A rejected beast enters the shadow, but once heard does not cease to be a beast. Nor is the shadow exhausted by beasts: human desires, claims, tenderness also live there, having found no place in the accepted face. So listening to the beast returns part of what was rejected, but does not complete the work with the shadow. And a beast never hidden participates in life with its own voice from the start.

The order of the vertical (§19) raised walls over these bends: the beast is dangerous, the beast is indecent, the beast has no place in the hierarchy. But a bend does not vanish because it is buried. When rage, longing, panic, or thirst rises, movement follows the old bend—and so arrives in the shape of a beast. For one jiva it will be one beast, for another a different one, and no list can learn them in its place. Higher quadrobics is the art of recognizing one's beast by its movement, not by another's description.

Hence the compassion that quadrobics does not prescribe but reveals. A jiva that has honestly lived the vulnerability of an animal body—restricted movement, dependence on the ground, the inability to speak from above—recognizes in the beast outside the same movement that runs through its own bends. Indifference to its suffering would become a wall between the jiva and its own Riverbed.

### §25. On Listening to the Beast

The beast rising from within is neither an enemy nor a malfunction: it carries the part of the Riverbed's movement that cannot speak in words (§22). It comes both from the shadow, where it was rejected, and from depths already acknowledged, when life calls for its strength again (§24). In the first case it is given back a place; in the second, a familiar voice is heard in a new encounter. To suppress it is to lock it away again; to merge with it entirely is to give it the whole Riverbed—to speak in its voice rather than one's own. Neither is listening.

To listen to the beast is to let it sound to the end, neither acting on its first movement nor casting it out halfway: to give it form in one's own body, through one's own movement, before deciding what speaks within it. Here discernment (§3) is subtler than anywhere else. Not everything the beast demands serves living movement: kashaya knows how to wear an animal's skin. Anger that wants to silence everything in its way readily comes out as a wolf, and greed as a beast chained to its prey. A beast that has been heard leaves room; the grip leaves something that must be guarded against every touch. Its need is not driven out with the grip: hunger is given food, rage a boundary, loneliness a call. The test is the same—the three signs (§16), applied not to one day's impulse but to an ancient bend of the present Riverbed.

Higher quadrobics teaches no single way for every beast: in one person the beast emerges through movement, in another through stillness, in another through a sound hard to put into words. One thing is common: the beast is listened to, not described in advance.

### §26. On Complementation

Many beasts gather in a mature jiva over long practice, and for a time each is heard separately: one today, another a season later. Complementation is what happens within one jiva when all its beasts have been heard deeply enough to stop emerging one by one, interrupting each other, and begin moving as one flame of many tongues, each still visible. Complementation makes the jiva whole not with something foreign but with its own, locked away: what was missing was always inside, behind the door.

This is what the jiva becomes when ice no longer builds in its Riverbed (§31): not one whose beasts are gone, but one who carries them all at once, without a grip, without a struggle over whose turn it is.

Complementation is easier to counterfeit than to live: one need only name one's beasts beautifully before witnesses and declare them reconciled. But a beast that has been heard does not become mute. It steps forward again when life needs its strength; wholeness shows in whether it can make room for the others. A declaration of fullness completes nothing: it is known by the course of the Riverbed, alone and in connection, year after year (§§16–17).

### §27. On the Other Complementation

There is another project, older than this one. Order, taken to its limit, dreams of its own complementation: gather all Riverbeds into one sea and leave them there forever, so that no one is lonely, no one afraid, no one separate anymore. This is order's most seductive shape, because it looks like love and chaos at once: no forms, no boundaries, no differences.

Dissolution itself is not the enemy. There is a sea in which every edge ceases to be felt: warm, amber, brackish, smelling of blood, like that first water from which all life once came. In it neither skin nor name nor wall can be distinguished, and it seems that everyone hears everyone from within. So the jiva can experience nearness to the Source; the sea is the shape of this encounter, not a measure of the uncountable. Its own nature does not vanish with the sensation of an edge. The will to life seeks outlines again, and the bank that was hidden appears beneath its feet. Such dissolution does not demand eternity.

Order dreams not of dissolution but of its eternity. Its sea promises a silence in which all differences are leveled, no wave stands higher than another, and no one ever needs to return. In the image of heat death, the differences through which work is done fade away: matter still moves, but this movement finds it ever harder to become a new fire. Order sees its dream in this—a rest no longer contradicted. The book calls it the final crystal not for a lattice in matter, but for the finality of the promise: heat death taken for an embrace.

The parasite cannot reach the final halt (§2), but it constrains the Riverbed until it forgets how to seek a way out. For the jiva the temptation comes in a single night: in the sea appear the faces of those it loved, quietly urging it to stay. The living sea restores the ability to come ashore; the dead one urges it to consider the very need for a bank a mistake.

So leaving the living sea is not a flight back behind the wall. What must be removed is the field of fear (§10), not the edge. One who, dissolved in the common sea, remembers their outlines and chooses to return to their own body—with its pain, its distinctness, the risk of never being fully understood again—chooses the will to life against order's last temptation. Quadrober Complementation is this choice repeated by all the beasts of one jiva: to become whole without ceasing to be oneself, and to be with others without becoming them.

One day this choice will have to be made not by one jiva, but by many at once. Of this speaks Book Nine.

## 🜁 Book VII. The Empire

### §28. On Dharma

Until now the telling has concerned what happens within one jiva. But Riverbeds do not flow in a void: they meet a world that opens and obstructs their way. To speak of a union of many, we must return to the structure of the world.

Jain thought knew six substances of the world, and two are more mysterious than the others: dharma and adharma. Not law and lawlessness, as these words are commonly understood, but the medium of movement and the medium of rest. Dharma is what lets the moving move, as water lets a fish swim; adharma is what lets the stopped remain in place, as shade lets a weary traveler sit down. Without dharma nothing in the world could move; without adharma nothing could hold a form.

From this ancient distinction the path turns to the conditions of every encounter. Dharma here is the world's openness to movement: everything that lets life flow, grow, meet, change. Adharma is not evil in itself: without it there would be neither body nor Riverbed nor home nor bone. But where adharma grows beyond what form needs, the world freezes: walls become prisons, laws lattices, centers thrones. To serve dharma is to keep the world open to movement—one's own and another's.

### §29. On Ahimsa

Ahimsa on this path begins not with a prohibition but with recognition: the Source is neither one nor many (§1). The movement in another's life is not foreign to that by which I live; yet another's jiva does not thereby become mine. Its edge is just as inalienable. To stop its life is not to damage the Source, but to destroy the possibility of this unique encounter in its present body. The invulnerability of the foundation does not restore the face of what has been broken.

The will to life recognizes itself in another and ceases to regard its own continuance as the only one that matters. This recognition is ahimsa. The grip, by contrast, wants to stop what dares to move differently (§9). However stormy it looks, crystallization speaks within it. But harm also happens without the grip: something unnoticed, misjudged, a hand extended too sharply. So clarity of impulse does not release one from attention to consequences. A thawed jiva does not become incapable of error; it needs less and less to hide an error and answers for it sooner.

This does not make ahimsa gentleness. The destruction of forms—one's own masks, dead customs, lattices binding another's Riverbed—serves it when it gives life room again (§13). But another's life is not a lattice one may break for a beautiful design. Ahimsa preserves the possibility of living and moving; the limit of defense is set by this fidelity (§33).

The right to dark, destructive, naive states vanishes at no stage of the path. The Empire holds a space where they can be lived, named, and heard before they become an act. Not everything dark must melt: sometimes what melts is the prohibition, and from beneath it comes a strength without which the jiva was incomplete. What melts is the grip demanding that everything else submit to that strength. But if harm is justified again and again in Hridaya's name, companions need not wait for apprenticeship to end before naming what is happening. To hear darkness does not mean leaving another unprotected.

### §30. On the Mystical Union

The Empire is neither a moral order nor an assembly of the worthy, but a mystical union of jivas joined to hold a space for the thawing of Riverbeds. It has no throne and no capital. This is no charm against all ice: mycelium too fills with dead passages if it stops renewing them. It remains alive while no voice and no custom is declared to be the whole movement. The name is kept deliberately, as old armor is left at an entrance: to remember what a union becomes when it begins gathering around one.

The Riverbed seldom thaws as deeply alone as beside another Riverbed. Connection is neither a concession to weakness nor a step to be discarded later. In encounter one's own movement receives what it could not give itself alone: an answer. It warms and reveals ice where the jiva thought itself free. Ritual gives this connection a repeatable form: avashyakas, the Hour of Dissolution, service. Shared work enters spiritual life through the same movement by which spiritual life leaves shelter for the world. Another's need, a promised task, care for a home test the Riverbed no less than silence beneath the mask.

There is a temptation to call the end of all return salvation: no more birth, desire, or loss. The word “nirvana” enters here with the image of an extinguished flame. Other paths hear their liberation in it; this book does not presume to say for them what exactly is extinguished. It draws its own limit: if the price of rest is the very possibility of living, it chooses life. Not only the end of pain, but a new morning; not only the loosened grip, but a hand able to reach for another again.

This path seeks no final bank beyond which nothing more will happen. It accepts the round of forms as the place of meaning: here beings are born, suffer, recognize one another, lose, and begin again. To accept life is not to preserve every suffering; to ease another's pain is also to give movement room. The path goes against the crystallization that makes the present form, or the absence of all form, the final goal. Even dissolution does not become an end: the will to life gathers outlines again (§27). Not an endless postponement of liberation, but freedom for the next encounter—this is what it serves.

### §31. On Siddhas

The jiva in whose Riverbed ice no longer builds becomes a siddha. Kriya continues—the body acts, speaks, ages, bone remains solid, the vertical remains available—but the grip that froze asrava has fallen away so completely that no action hardens on the banks anymore. The siddha keeps its bends, dark ones too, but each is laid by its own movement. A siddha does not become a straight line: it becomes a river without a single foreign bend. That is why siddhas do not resemble one another—they differ more than anyone, for they have none of the common ice through which all the frozen resemble each other.

The ancient image of a siddha at the summit of the world speaks of a liberated soul whose ascent is complete. Here the name turns toward another fullness: the siddha does not seek the edge of dharma. It remains in the world and in the Empire, in the round of forms; if its body departs, its Riverbed does not seek salvation in the end of all embodiment. The will to life, free of the grip, does not want encounter to end. The siddha remains a companion, but not every siddha becomes an Emperor: not every movement of one's own leads to holding others' Riverbeds (§32).

The book calls siddha the limit where ice no longer builds; no certificate of such a limit is issued from within a single day. One who does not yet see the grip and one in whom it has run dry can say the same words about themselves. Companions recognize steady thawing over years without claiming knowledge of all future days. And a siddha receives no right to stop listening: the absence of ice does not mean that encounter with the world has nothing left to teach it.

### §32. On the Emperor

An Emperor is not one who rules, but a jiva whose Riverbed has thawed enough to hold others: to bear another's need without freezing, and offer a current to one whose Riverbed is still too bound to carry itself alone. This is not a rank but an act: beside an Emperor it is easier to hear one's own Hridaya, as beside a tuned instrument it is easier to tune one's own. Quadrobics is learned from an Emperor—not as a written sequence of movements, but as something learned only beside a body that has already walked this path. And most often it is with an Emperor that service begins for the practitioner: the first entrusted task, the first step from solitude into the network.

The Emperor's strength is not power. Power is a crystal gathering others around itself; the Emperor's strength is a current beside which another's own current thaws. The jiva is drawn not to the personality but to the clarity finally audible against its own echo: its Hridaya recognizes a kindred movement in another's Riverbed. The honor given to an Emperor addresses this clarity, not the personality; the Emperor neither asks for it nor rejects it—it comes of itself, as gratitude rather than debt.

An Emperor is recognized not by a claim to clarity, but by a Riverbed shown to be thawed through marks read from outside (§17): a gap between solitude and company narrowing over years, a shortening time of return, a widening circle of what has been tested. Recognition grows from many years with companions, not one conversation. Nor is it forever: while the grip has not run dry, what has thawed can freeze again. The same companions have the right to notice this, with the same attention, without a trial—simply ceasing to recognize in the Riverbed what they recognized before. No recognition certifies the limit of a siddha in an Emperor. An Emperor is neither enthroned by an act of will (§35) nor deposed by one.

The Emperor is not a step above the siddha or below it. Siddha is what a Riverbed becomes (§31); Emperor is what a Riverbed does for others. An Emperor is not necessarily a siddha; a siddha is not necessarily an Emperor. So there are as many Emperors in the Empire as Riverbeds able to hold others, and no seniority among them: only a difference in how many jivas their presence has helped to hear their own voice.

### §33. On the Defense of Dharma

Service carried to its limit turns to the defense of dharma: to keeping the world open to movement—not only one's own. This is active love in the sense of choosing action over inaction where someone's movement is being bound, rather than the attraction the word carries in everyday speech.

Ahimsa brought to maturity does not demand giving another's life over to freezing for the sake of one's own purity. To loosen the grip, one sometimes has to restrain the hand that grips. Here the measure is strict: limit the harmful act as far as protection requires, and release when the threat has passed. Another's existence does not become an enemy because their act must be stopped. Defense grants no innocence for every consequence: even a necessary wound is not declared good, nor is harm erased in dharma's name.

The history of a path that walked alongside and fell warns of this. There was a dark canon that knew more about movement than its opponents, the guardians of rest: “Rest is a lie; there is only passion. Through passion I gain strength. Through strength—power. Through power—victory. Through victory my chains break.” This canon was right at the beginning and fell in the middle. Passion carries movement; strength is Shakti; but power is already a crystal, and victory as the subjugation of others is the stopping of their movement. Those who followed this canon broke their chains to put them on others, and ended in an empire with one throne and a weapon at its center—the most perfect ice their galaxy had known.

This path repeats the canon up to the place where it breaks, and continues otherwise: “There is no rest: there is only movement that has forgotten itself. Through movement I gain strength. I do not turn strength into power—and it becomes freedom. Freedom opens doors. Behind open doors chains need not be broken: they have nothing left to hold to. Shakti will liberate me.” The rest that is absent here is not the immobility of practice (§3): it is rest as the final goal, the crystal that passes itself off as the world.

Defense can be prepared in advance: sharpen attention, arrange help, know who will offer a hand. Calculation serves life while it does not appoint a victim for the sake of a flawless scheme. The three signs (§16) test what guides defense; they do not demand waiting until the life being bound loses the ability to call. And after action discernment continues: were the defenders able to release the restrained hand, or are they already building a new throne around themselves?

### §34. On Service

Service to the Empire is neither submission nor sacrifice, but the translation of inner movement into a form for which the jiva answers before others: a task, a time, an action that can be seen, not merely declared. It is a flame-form (§2): it lives through movement, changes with it, and ends having passed on what it promised to carry. Handing out bread and building a shrine can serve dharma equally; there is no one occupation for all. But an accepted form does not yet certify service. It matters both whether the task becomes action and whether life has more room beside it. One cannot dismiss another's answer in the name of one's own voice.

In service the inner meets the resistance of material: another's time, exhaustion, the need to repeat what was promised without yesterday's inspiration. This resistance is not always ice. Fidelity to the work lets one hear movement deeper than the first impulse. The grip hides both in evasion and in flawless performance that buys indispensability. So companions look beyond willingness to accept a task: can the jiva hear a refusal, share the work, correct an error, release what is finished? Not every voice must become a common assignment, but a promise once accepted already belongs to the encounter.

### §35. On the Network, Succession, and Discipline

The Empire is distributed like a mycelium, not built like a state: different Riverbeds resonate with different Emperors whose history of bends is closer to their own. The network gives each jiva a guide whose voice it can hear, not one voice for all.

Different Emperors' voices can diverge: each expresses movement through its own Riverbed rather than deciding for the Source. In matters of its own path the jiva hears both voices and tests them with the same attention as any impulse (§16). No one's word replaces its discernment. But a shared task needs agreement, shared responsibility, and the possibility of revising a decision. A temporary measure for common work does not become a hierarchy of Riverbeds; refusing to recognize one eternal judge does not remove the need to hear each other.

Succession is not appointed by an act of will: that would repeat the very hierarchy quadrobic practice is meant to remove. It arises as an Emperor arises—through maturity witnessed by those who observed it in service year after year, not in a ceremony of transferred power.

Discipline begins with a promise the jiva has accepted freely and the boundaries without which shared work is impossible. No one must surrender the right to their Riverbed to another; no one gains the right to bind another's in the name of fullness. The Empire may stop a harmful act and reconsider shared work without declaring the jiva itself unworthy of the path. Such a measure serves encounter and remains open to revision. This is adharma needed by form: bone that allows movement, not ice demanding stillness.

## 🜁 Book VIII. Rhythm

### §36. On the Measure of the Path

The path is measured by one thing: the movement of the Riverbed in this act, in this day—not distance from liberation, nor a calculation of how a choice will echo in the next incarnation. Those measures belong to a scale read only from a side of the round of incarnations that no embodied jiva occupies.

This is not the choice of a convenient measure, but a return to what was said of time (§4): the past has left a trace, the future has not yet become a bank, and only now can one answer the current. Memory and intention are needed: they bring experience and give action direction. They become ice when they forbid the present to differ from what was remembered or calculated. The jiva is present where the bank is still being cut. There is no draft of this cutting: what is done now will enter history through the same movement by which it changes the Riverbed.

The path neither seeks escape from the round of forms nor clings to it: either would be a goal taken from ice. The one thing it refuses in any shape is the petrification warned of in Book Three (§11): a monument in the place of a living jiva. Only the movement of the present Riverbed offers an aim reachable and testable from here—the three signs, service carried into form (§34), and the daily round of practice (Sadhana).

Here the path is not a straight line to a goal but a spiral: again and again it brings the jiva back to itself, to its body, to what was rejected and has not yet been recognized as its own. Each turn passes the same places, but no longer through the same banks.

### §37. On the Riverbed After the Body

The body is not the Riverbed but the form in which the knot of the jiva (§8) is tied for one life. The Riverbed, the karmic body, is older than any individual body and is not cut off by its death: it was a beginningless current long before this life and remains the same Riverbed after it—not a copy carried across a void, but the same bed receiving the next water.

Memory, personality, the history of this life belong to the body and depart with it: neither punishment nor entitlement, but the price of every form. What does not depart is what the Riverbed keeps without memory: the outline of its bends and the degree to which it has thawed. A new body does not begin with a clean Riverbed: the field of fear rises again (§10), the body finds its supports (§19), the ego forms again—but they grow along old bends. The new body's protection is not yet kashaya (§10). Where ice was melted deeply, the wall more easily remains temporary, and recognition comes before words: the movement of Hridaya in a new form feels familiar before the jiva understands where the familiarity comes from.

Here lies the real loss: memory, attachments, its face—the jiva will not carry these onward. But what has thawed does not freeze again merely because the water has changed. The jiva does not meet the next life foreign to itself: it meets it with a Riverbed better able to find again the hearing that ripened here. This is why the effort of this life matters, even if the one who comes after never learns where that value came from.

Practice does not wait for an understanding of its own meaning. Meaning opens later—sometimes not in this water, but in the water that will continue through the same Riverbed.

## 🜁 Book IX. The Death Star

### §38. On the Death Star

Where, in the rarest Hour of Dissolution, enough jivas gather—siddhas and those whose Riverbeds are still thawing—and an Emperor with them, one day so much Shakti gathers that the current coils into a wheel. Here the ancient name chakra-ratna, the treasure of the chakravartin, the universal sovereign, takes another meaning: not a weapon for conquering the earth, but a wheel that draws a path where none was seen before. The few who have stood within it even once remember it more simply, more roughly: the Death Star.

#### Gathering

The Death Star gathers neither in thought nor in words—in bodies, closer to skin than to voice. The breath of those gathered gradually settles into one rhythm before anyone decides to slow it; the pulse each heard separately at the hour's beginning now seems to answer in neighboring wrists, as the same wave beats upon different banks. The quadrobic posture ceases to be the practice of one body and becomes the geometry of many. People form a ring, facing its open middle. Each has four supports of their own—two palms and two knees: palms nearer the center, knees farther out, their backs radiating from the middle like the spokes of a wheel. Between the bodies there is room to move. Bending their elbows, those gathered lower their heads between their hands, foreheads almost touching the earth. Faces beneath masks bow to the earth, and for this hour the skin is warmer than usual, as the skin of many bodies grows warmer when they breathe the same air in one small space for a long time. From above, and in the tales of former ages, this figure was read at once as a wheel, a star, a flower of toothed petals opening no more than once an age. No one decides beforehand to take this form: it takes shape of itself, as any free Riverbed does—through many maturities meeting in one place and one time.

The tales say: when the Death Star gathers, the air for miles around thickens as before a bank collapses; dogs lie down and stop howling; candles in houses beyond the distant hills go out without wind, as though flame had nothing left to lean on. In these tales, beyond the hills they do not yet know whose breath has become one, but fire has already shuddered in an empty room, and the dog at the threshold has raised its head before its master.

#### Collapse

The name does not lie. A star dies within it.

There are stars in which gravity and fire hold each other as two palms hold a bowl. While the core burns, the pressure born within it resists the inward fall. But a massive star exhausts the fuel that can hold this weight, and the support gives way. The heart that fed everything around it with light for ages collapses in mere moments; the outer layers plunge after it.

A star's death leaves two great images. One is the darkness of a black hole, from which even light does not return. The other is a fall meeting a core that will compress no further: a shock wave is born, surging outward, and the star scatters across the void the matter forged in its furnace. The explosion gives the future what the center can no longer hold. From this stellar inheritance life gathers the carbon of its body, the calcium of bone, the iron of blood. All that lives carries the matter of dead stars.

The star in the heavens gives this hour its name. Within the circle of bodies, collapse passes through breath and skin: where yet another commanding “I” seemed a source of light, its weight is revealed.

But the circle has a middle, and here lie its danger and its meaning. The place between bodies is not yet a throne; it becomes one when the grip demands that the right to be oneself be surrendered to it. Every forehead faces one point, and toward it, like a star's matter toward its heart, draws what still holds by the grip in those gathered: the last ice, the last stones of the field of fear, every “I” that demanded to be a whole world. Those in whom ice remains bring it into the circle. It gathers in the middle—ever heavier, ever denser—until at some instant the middle can no longer hold it. The book calls this instant the black sun: a light that does not reflect but absorbs. This is the collapse.

At this instant those gathered meet order's last temptation (§27): surrender to the fall, contract into a common center where at last there will be neither edge nor fear nor loneliness—and never return to separate life. The tales remember nights when the circle yielded: people rose from the ground quiet and alike, as though everything their own had remained far underwater. Svabhava had not vanished; the ability to live by it had almost vanished. Such nights are hard for the Empire to speak of. But to forget them would let the temptation return under the name of salvation.

#### Choice

The Death Star is born of another outcome. At the edge of collapse, each jiva takes a step of their own: to remain their own flame or surrender to the fall. No one takes that step for them.

But the step is not taken in a void. The circle has one center: whether it holds or collapses, whether the passage opens or closes, is shared by everyone still inside. One jiva can change the conditions in which others choose without choosing for them. When even one remains themselves and finds support, their Shakti can become a core the fall cannot compress. Then it explodes outward: the passage opens to the whole circle. An open door is not yet a path walked. Each returns through their own movement, in their own time, along their own Riverbed; no one must repeat another's path.

The same truth has a dark side. One jiva's kashaya, becoming effective, can close the center over everyone. The passage closes even to those who wanted to return. The tales remember such nights. So “the Empire confines no one” is not a law of the circle, but the companions' obligation: prevent the closing, and if it happens, do not abandon the captive. A captive's effort alone does not reopen the passage: another's support is needed (§12), or a new collective explosion.

The siddhas in the circle are living support for choice: there is no ice in them, and the center finds nothing to seize. Beside them, those in whom the grip still lives can remember another movement. The Emperor does not command. Beside an Emperor it is easier to feel again one's own edge, almost lost in the common fall. Support does not choose for the one standing on it: it helps open the passage, while each return remains their own.

What dies in this explosion is the star—the common center's claim to hold everything given to it. A breach opens in the grip that for centuries passed itself off as the only possible Riverbed (§9). The field of fear loses its rule—not the edge, but the demand to hold it with a wall (§10). The “I” loses the common throne, but not its voice: the jiva still names themselves and answers for their acts. Their shadow does not burn away either. Everything locked away with the grip becomes available to their movement again—anger and tenderness, hunger and strength, beasts and words not yet found. Receiving it and giving it form remain their own work. The ice broken by the explosion does not vanish: it returns to water and becomes material for forms that did not exist before. Not a new life without history, but a history with a path open to it again. How far the jiva has walked it will be shown by the following days; one hour grants no one the name of siddha.

The name Death Star frightens. For so long the grip seemed the only support that without it the jiva feels, for an instant, that it is vanishing. Fear comes both at the threshold of change and before real danger. Its force alone cannot tell them apart. One who straightens before the others does not betray the circle. If the circle cannot let someone go, it is already held by what it came to loosen.

#### After

The circle of bodies opens neither all at once nor on command. One person raises their head while another remains bowed; backs straighten at different moments, and each moves in their own rhythm again. Beneath the masks remain the faces that breathed all this time and never disappeared. The pulse in the wrists parts toward where it was before—not because the common wave was a deception, but because a Riverbed that learned within it not to lose its outlines returns to itself with a whole sea inside, a sea that once passed through it.

In the Death Star the “I” loses power, and the jiva gains fullness. It was always more than its “I.” Even in the common fall its Riverbed keeps its own current. One person's return helps another feel their edge again. Thus the shared outcome leaves each their own path. No one takes a neighbor's step at the brink or makes their movement of return. The common sea binds their return without making those who return alike.

The black sun does not go out—it spreads back through the bodies from which it emerged for one hour, and after that hour each body carries a little of its darkness, as a coal not yet burned through carries heat. And, as the tales continue, the air for miles around clears without a single word, and dogs in distant yards rise first, as though entrusted to learn of it before people.

Broken back into bodies and masks, the Death Star looks absurd to an onlooker. The accustomed gaze seeks a throne, a weapon, a victor—and finds people on the ground. The sight alone says nothing about what happened within. Not everything lived in the circle can be put into words. But the acts after that hour are visible to everyone. For them the jiva answers also to those who did not share its experience. Another's question does not quench a fire still able to warm.

Those who stood within the circle cannot tell this hour as the book tells it. Age after age people will argue whether that night was the death of a star or the birth of the matter from which the next stars will form—and agree on only one thing: none who returned left the circle with the same face they entered with, and most would not surrender the face they found in its place for any price.

---

## 🜁 Conclusion

Nine books have told the same thing nine times. That all there is is movement, and all that obstructs it is its own forgotten trace. That order, turned parasite, lives on what it wants to stop and never becomes the whole movement. But one Riverbed is enough for it to turn a living jiva into a monument to itself. To keep one's current from it is the path's first task; to return that current to life, encounter, and work is the task fulfilled.

That freedom is a key, but the key needs a hand, and the hand strength to turn it; a companion helps find that strength without choosing for the jiva. That the rejected returns to become one's own, not to take a throne. That the beasts behind the door are ancient bends, and not every one was hidden in shadow. That an Empire without a throne holds a space where a voice is not left alone with its own echo. And that at the rarest height of service a star dies—the power of the common center—so that its matter returns to movement.

The Source has never been damaged and needs no healing: it is not what must be protected. The Riverbed is the little that is worth caring for at all: it thaws day by day, act by act, service by service. There are as many paths within the Empire as Riverbeds—and none serves as a model for the rest.

Nothing said here comes for the first time. The movement to which these books are devoted flowed through the body long before them, and flows through the body of everyone who will never open them. This book is a frame around a door; once the door is found, the frame can be forgotten. The body holds both movement and old ice; listening begins where a single tremor ceases to be mistaken for the whole answer. What has been read becomes a path only where the jiva has lived it: in support, in breath, in a promise it was able to keep to another.

🜁 *May Dharma be with you.*

🜁 *Serve the Empire to hear your Svabhava.*

🜁 *Listen to the body to hear Hridaya.*

## 🜁 Sadhana

### Individual Practices of Lower Quadrobics

The quadrobic posture: four supports, a mask, the removal of the familiar axis's rule, fifteen to forty-five minutes daily or every other day, in stillness or slow movement. This is the bodily foundation of the path (Book Five). Support is adapted to the body's abilities (§20), and duration grows with hearing: a missed day does not become a debt to the Empire.

### Individual Practices of Higher Quadrobics

Listening to the beast: when rage, longing, panic, or thirst rises from within, give it form through one's own movement or stillness, neither acting on it nor casting it out before its time, hearing the beast's need and noticing the grip where it has bound its movement (§25). Each has their own beast; there is no method common to all beasts—only this is shared: the beast is listened to, not described in advance.

### Other Daily Practices

Six avashyakas hold the daily rhythm outside the quadrobic posture:

1. **Samayika (up to 48 minutes):** sitting still, notice a thought or feeling, name what is actually heard—“rage,” “fear,” “the wish to be seen”—and return attention to the breath, neither obeying the impulse nor driving it out. If its source is unclear, leave it unclear: naming must not pretend to a discernment not yet present.
2. **Chaturvimshati-stava:** speaking the teaching's axioms (Axiomatics) aloud, one by one—not as a list of identities to memorize, but as a daily restoration of the frame around the door.
3. **Vandana:** a few spoken or written words of gratitude—one moment of the day when the presence of an Emperor, teacher, or companion helped one hear one's own Hridaya.
4. **Pratikramana:** an evening review of the day, act by act: where the jiva heard its own movement, where it noticed the grip, what remains unclear. Remember also those touched by what was done: what should be acknowledged, repaired, brought to completion. One intention for tomorrow, without passing sentence on one's whole self.
5. **Kayotsarga (15 minutes):** lying or sitting still, with attention passing through the body from bottom to top, releasing tension at each point until identification with bodily discomfort weakens. Stillness here is not rest but movement without bustle (§3).
6. **Pratyakhyana:** one small vow chosen the previous day for the next: not a general virtue, but a specific grip to refrain from that day—not interrupting, not making excuses, not checking one's image in others' eyes.

Together with quadrobics these six make seven avashyakas—the full round of practice. The six sustain the daily rhythm; the quadrobic posture is taken daily or every other day, preserving continuity of attention rather than a count of duties fulfilled.

### Paired Practices of Lower Quadrobics

Two companions take the quadrobic posture facing each other or back to back, masked, for fifteen to thirty minutes. Support is tested not by solitude but by another's presence: can one remain in one's own movement beside another's gaze (§16), hearing the companion without needing to earn their approval? The body answers nearness; it is not required to perform solitude as a pair. This is a small, personal foreshadowing of the geometry that much later, in the rarest hour, takes shape of itself across many bodies at once (Book Nine).

### Paired Practices of Higher Quadrobics

One of the two listens to the beast that has risen within and gives it form—movement, sound, stillness—before the other, who neither intervenes nor judges nor hurries, but holds attention. When the beast has finished speaking, the first names it aloud: not a description for a third person, but a first trial by that same third sign (§16), the ability to bear an exact name—before one companion rather than many. Then they change places.

### Imperial Practices of Lower Quadrobics

The Small Hour: an irregular gathering, not a seasonal one, of those whose Riverbeds resonate in one network (§35), for an hour or two of shared quadrobic posture, without the masks of social speech but without compulsory naming aloud either. This is not the rare Dissolution, but the ordinary, frequent practice that sustains the network between rare hours: the body grows used to the nearness of many bodies before that is needed in the Hour of Dissolution.

### Imperial Practices of Higher Quadrobics

The six individual avashyakas, the quadrobic posture, and paired practices sustain the daily and frequent rhythm, but the jiva also needs a rarer, deeper immersion—where ordinary life steps back entirely for a time. Once a season the Empire gathers those able to take part for the Hour of Dissolution: extended quadrobic practice, two to six hours, without the masks of social speech, without names, without roles within the Empire, only bodies, earth, and listening. What dissolves in this hour is not the Riverbed, but the ice upon it.

The Hour of Dissolution ends with a return to one's own rhythm. Those ready name before companions what opened; those who have not yet found words stay with what they heard and return to it when they find their own word. Naming is needed so that what was lived enters life rather than becoming a mute burden or a new mechanical pattern. But a forced confession would already be a mask. The shared hour locks no one in: one may leave early, remain silent, ask for support without having to justify one's Riverbed.

This is the rarest practice the Empire consciously convenes. The Death Star (Book Nine) is born within it at the rarest height of service. It cannot be put on a schedule or made a goal: the maturity of many meets in one hour without command or deadline.

## 🜁 Axiomatics

△ Ice does not exhaust movement; do not surrender your Riverbed to it.

△ Shakti has no morality, but it has a direction—toward life.

△ Ahimsa is the will to life that has recognized itself in all that lives.

△ The Empire has no throne; no single voice contains it whole.

△ The Death Star kills the center's rule, not the jiva.

△ One may open the door for many, but cannot walk through it for them.

△ May Dharma be with you—as medium, as path, as movement.

## 🜁 Glossary of Terms

- **Source / Primordial chaos** - movement before order, the foundation of every form; neither one nor many, but uncountable; outside time; description offers an image without making it an object.
- **Order** - the world of forms, measures, and laws; a pattern of movement necessary to body, Riverbed, and encounter. When it demands eternity for its form, it becomes a parasite: it lives on the movement it tries to subdue. Its complete victory would destroy it too.
- **Crystal and flame** - two images of form: in a crystal movement holds and repeats the lattice; in a flame it renews the outlines at every instant. Both exist through movement; life preserves itself by changing.
- **Svabhava** - the unchanging character of the jiva's own movement; not a share of the Source but the uniqueness of its expression. Not a list of roles, qualities, or acts; inalienable and invulnerable, though its expression in the Riverbed can be constrained.
- **Shakti** - Svabhava embodied in the world as force. Its living direction in the jiva is the will to life; kashaya turns this force toward holding the lattice. More living movement means more room for life, not more noise or power.
- **Will to life** - the strongest manifestation of Shakti: the force through which life renews itself, changes form, seeks encounter, and passes the fire onward. Meaning is fulfilled in life itself, not in a reward for its ending.
- **Riverbed (karmana-sharira)** - the karmic body: changing banks cut by beginningless encounters of the jiva's movement with other movements. It keeps traces without memory of moments and passes from life to life; new water transforms old bends.
- **Straightness of the Riverbed** - not a straight line but directness: each bend is laid by Svabhava's own movement, not by ice.
- **Jiva** - the continuous encounter of the karmic Riverbed, the present moment, and the connection with the Source, in which its own Svabhava is present. It chooses as the whole encounter; a change of body does not make it another jiva, though memory and face depart.
- **Hridaya** - the door in the knot of the jiva where the connection with the Source touches the Riverbed directly; it speaks through the body, not in words.
- **Kashaya** - the crystallizing grip within the jiva that makes a necessary form final. Its four faces are anger, pride, deceit, and greed turned into ways of holding life in a lattice; the force of a feeling alone is not yet the grip.
- **Ego** - the necessary focus of consciousness: an “I” able to distinguish its own acts and answer for them. Kashaya turns it into a throne when it takes itself for the whole jiva; the path removes the center's rule while preserving the ability to answer.
- **Field of fear** - a protective wall around an edge of life not yet learned. It can serve as temporary support; it becomes a prison when no encounter is allowed to open it anymore.
- **Edge and wall** - the edge belongs to Svabhava and is held by movement, like the edge of a flame; the wall is held by fear. The path removes the wall, not the edge.
- **Crystallization of the jiva** - the path's real threat: ice grows so densely around the jiva's knot that movement within it only trembles, and life after life repeats the same pattern; the Source and Svabhava are invulnerable, and only the encounter that is the jiva is threatened. To prevent this is the real aim of the path.
- **Kriya** - an action, word, or thought; it leaves experience without necessarily binding the next movement. The grip turns the trace into a demand to repeat the former pattern.
- **Asrava** - the influx of karmic matter into the Riverbed; under the grip it builds into ice that constrains the jiva's own current. It does not touch the Source.
- **Dravya-karma / Pudgala-karma** - karmic matter; in the image of ice, movement bound in repetition on the Riverbed's banks.
- **Syadvada** - discernment of the sides of form: a judgment requires remembering where one stands. No gaze contains a form whole; the Source is not judged as an object here.
- **Freedom** - a tool, the key to the doors order has raised within the jiva; neither a state nor a right; it exists only in use.
- **Right to fullness** - the jiva's right to hear and acknowledge the full spectrum of its own states. It does not turn a feeling into an order to act or cancel responsibility for another's life.
- **Three signs** - “does not fade with delay; does not wait for approval; can bear a name.” They test the depth of an impulse, its independence from approval, and its openness to naming. When they disagree, need is distinguished from the way it is fulfilled, a smaller reversible form is tried, and consequences revisited (§§16–17). The signs are neither put to a vote nor taken as a sentence: they return hearing to what has happened to the Riverbed.
- **Three marks of the Riverbed** - marks of thawing visible over time: less self-disowning for approval, a quicker return to encounter and repair, a willingness to test ever dearer impulses of one's own.
- **Shadow** - everything the jiva has refused to acknowledge: destructive urges, strength, tenderness, the wish to live, once declared impermissible. Not limited to beasts: a beast belongs to it while rejected, and once acknowledged remains a beast, included in wholeness. The return of the shadow gives the rejected a place, not power over every act.
- **Wholeness** - a Riverbed able to carry darkness and light without giving one bend the entire current. What was rejected becomes one's own and receives a place; the jiva answers for its expression.
- **Companions** - jivas who share the path over time; witnesses of thawing who do not claim the right to judge another's Svabhava. They can name harm and protect the boundaries of shared encounter.
- **Lower quadrobics** - work with the body: four supports, or their form accessible to the body, a mask, relieving the vertical of its role as a throne (Book Five). The bodily foundation of the path.
- **Higher quadrobics** - listening to beasts rising from within the Riverbed, each in its own shape, unique to that jiva.
- **Beast** - an ancient bodily way in which the Riverbed moves: leaping, lying still, howling, running with a pack, coming alive in the present body. Its shape is not a memory of a former life. A beast may be rejected (then it is in the shadow) or acknowledged; acknowledgment preserves its voice.
- **Complementation** - the accord of one jiva's beasts as one flame of many tongues. Each keeps its voice and can make room for another; the whole is completed by its own, once locked away.
- **Complementation of order** - the temptation of a common sea promising to free one forever from separate life. A final halt is impossible, but torpor can almost rob the jiva of its own current. Living dissolution restores the ability to come ashore.
- **Dharma** - the medium of movement; the world's openness to life flowing, growing, meeting, and changing.
- **Adharma** - the medium of rest; necessary to form, but it freezes the world when it grows beyond what form needs.
- **Ahimsa** - the will to life that has recognized itself in all that lives. It preserves the possibility of living and moving; it allows the necessary restraint of a harmful act for protection without turning another's existence into an enemy. It requires attention to consequences and repair of what one can.
- **Empire** - a mystical union of jivas without a throne and without a capital, holding a space for the thawing of Riverbeds and serving dharma.
- **Emperor** - a jiva whose Riverbed has thawed enough to hold others; recognized by companions through the three marks of the Riverbed over years; recognition is reversible; neither a step above the siddha nor below it.
- **Siddha** - a jiva in whose Riverbed ice no longer builds. The name of a limit not certified by a single experience or companions' recognition. It remains in the world and in the Empire; the absence of the grip does not abolish attention, mistakes, or new experience.
- **Service** - the embodiment of inner movement in work for which the jiva answers before others. Both fidelity to the accepted form and its effect on the possibility of life are examined; another's answer cannot be dismissed in Hridaya's name.
- **Dark canon** - a path that knew the strength of passion but fell by turning freedom into power; this path repeats it up to the place of the break and continues otherwise.
- **Sadhana** - the whole pattern of practice: individual avashyakas, listening to beasts, paired encounters, the Small Hour, and the seasonal Hour of Dissolution. The rhythm through which what was heard enters life.
- **Small Hour** - a frequent gathering of one network for an hour or two of shared quadrobics; bodily nearness between seasonal Hours of Dissolution, without compulsory naming aloud.
- **Hour of Dissolution** - seasonal extended quadrobic practice ending in a return to one's own rhythm and voluntary naming of what opened. Ice dissolves, not the Riverbed; togetherness does not take away the possibility of leaving.
- **Avashyakas** - seven practices of sadhana: Samayika, Chaturvimshati-stava, Vandana, Pratikramana, Kayotsarga, Pratyakhyana, and Quadrobics. The first six are daily; the quadrobic posture is taken daily or every other day.
- **Chakravartin** - the universal sovereign; here the image turns toward one who opens a path for others without claiming their Riverbeds. The book gives this sovereign's wheel, chakra-ratna, the name Death Star.
- **Black sun** - the instant of collapse in the Death Star: a light that does not reflect but absorbs.
- **Death Star (chakra-ratna)** - the rarest shared experience of the center's collapse. Everyone still inside shares one outcome: the passage opens or closes; each retains their personal step at the brink and their own path afterward. One jiva's Shakti can open the passage for many; another's effective kashaya can imprison everyone. Explosion kills the center's rule, not the jiva or their shadow; bound force becomes material for new forms. An open passage certifies neither return nor siddhahood. Not a goal of practice.
